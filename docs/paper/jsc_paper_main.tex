\title{Rank-Optimal Directional Taylor Jets for High-Order\\
       Mixed Derivatives of Neural Surrogates}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
High-order partial differential equation losses often require repeated
evaluation of one prescribed mixed derivative of a neural surrogate. We
develop a deterministic, algebraically exact procedure for this task. A
mixed derivative is first identified with a coefficient functional of the
homogeneous polynomial formed by directional Taylor coefficients. Constructing
a shortest directional formula is therefore equivalent to decomposing the
associated monomial into powers of linear forms. The
Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita rank formula gives the minimum number of complex
directions, and a roots-of-unity construction attains that minimum without
solving a linear system. We evaluate each direction by Taylor-mode propagation
through a multilayer perceptron and use a custom vector--Jacobian product so
that derivatives of the loss with respect to network parameters remain
available. Double-precision tests agree with nested automatic differentiation
for both values and parameter gradients. As an application, we train
complex-parameter \(\sinh\) physics-informed neural networks on 12
polyharmonic, radial-chirp, and time-harmonic Maxwell settings. Under a common
1200\,s, five-seed, width-128 protocol, this model has the lowest mean relative
\(L^2\) error in all 12 settings, although the margin is negligible for the
hardest three-dimensional sixth-order case. The comparison is deliberately
protocol-specific because trainable real parameter counts differ. The rank
reduction applies to a fixed repeated-index derivative; it gives no global
optimality result for general operator sums and no reduction for square-free
mixed derivatives.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Physics-informed neural network; high-order
derivative; Taylor-mode automatic differentiation; Waring decomposition;
apolarity; complex-valued neural network.
\end{abstract}

% =========================================================
\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Motivation and related work}

Scientific machine-learning models frequently require derivatives of a
neural surrogate with respect to its inputs. In a physics-informed neural
network (PINN) \cite{Raissi2019}, for example, each optimization step
evaluates the differential operator in the governing equation at many
collocation points. Fourth- and sixth-order equations can therefore make
input differentiation a substantial part of the training cost.

A direct implementation applies reverse-mode automatic differentiation
successively, retaining the graph after every coordinate derivative. The
depth and storage of this nested graph grow with both the derivative order
and the network depth. Three complementary strategies reduce this cost:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Forward- and Taylor-mode automatic differentiation}
\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008}, which propagates truncated Taylor
coefficients through each primitive without nesting input-gradient graphs;
\item \textbf{Stochastic estimators} \cite{Hu2023sdgd,Shi2024stde},
which write the target operator as an expectation over random tangent
vectors and approximate it by sampling; and
\item \textbf{Operator-specific specializations}
\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian,Gao2023folx} for the Laplacian (the trace
of the Hessian), exploiting the fact that the trace collapses cleanly
through linear layers.
\end{enumerate}
Taylor mode supplies a derivative along any chosen direction, but does not
specify a shortest set of directions for extracting one mixed partial.
Stochastic methods trade work for variance, whereas trace-specialized
methods address a different operator structure. Here we consider the
deterministic fixed-multi-index problem and identify its directional
schedule with the Waring decomposition of a monomial.

\subsection{Contributions and scope}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item We prove that a directional formula for a fixed mixed derivative exists
with \(R\) probes if and only if the associated degree-\(p\) monomial has a
Waring decomposition with \(R\) terms. Thus the minimum probe count is a
Waring rank (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).
\item The Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita theorem \cite{CCG2012} and a
roots-of-unity filter give a closed-form schedule that attains the complex
rank. Repeated-index patterns such as \((4,2)\) and \((2,2,2)\) require,
respectively, 5 rather than 7 and 9 rather than 13 directions compared with
coalesced real polarization.
\item We combine this schedule with Taylor-mode propagation through smooth
multilayer perceptrons and a custom vector--Jacobian product. The resulting
implementation is algebraically exact, differentiable with respect to model
parameters, and verified against nested automatic differentiation
(Sections~\ref{sec:impl} and~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item We apply the implementation to complex-parameter \(\sinh\) PINNs and
report five-seed, fixed-budget comparisons on polyharmonic, radial-chirp, and
time-harmonic Maxwell problems (Section~\ref{sec:osc}). The study records
accuracy, throughput, and trainable real degrees of freedom rather than
equating a common hidden width with parameter-count matching.
\end{enumerate}

The rank statement is deliberately narrow. It concerns one monomial
differential operator at a time. A sum such as \(\Delta^m\) can be expanded
and evaluated term by term; the schedule may shorten individual repeated
mixed terms, but it does not exploit cancellations or shared structure across
the sum. A trace-collapsing method may therefore be preferable when only a
Laplacian is required. Square-free derivatives receive no rank reduction, and
we make no claim of a universal wall-clock advantage over real polarization.
Stochastic estimators remain preferable when a controlled approximation of a
large tensor contraction is sufficient.

\subsection{Notation}

Throughout the paper, \(u:\R^{d}\to\R\) denotes a sufficiently smooth
scalar field. Let
\(\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d})\in\Z_{\ge 0}^{d}\) be a multi-index of
order \(p=|\nu|\), with
\begin{equation}
\dal
\;:=\;
\partial_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\partial_{d}^{\nu_{d}}.
\end{equation}
The implementation represents the same operator by an expanded coordinate
list in which coordinate \(k\) occurs \(\nu_k\) times.

Relabel the \emph{active coordinates} \(\{k:\nu_k>0\}\) as
\(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}\), and set \(a_j=\nu_{i_j}\). We order them so that
\(1\le a_{0}\le\cdots\le a_{n}\) (ties may be broken by the original
coordinate index). This relabeling singles out a minimum-exponent variable
\(i_0\), as required by the rank formula. Then
\begin{equation}
p \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n} a_{j},
\qquad
\nu! \;=\; \prod_{k=1}^{d}\nu_k!
             \;=\; \prod_{j=0}^{n} a_{j}!,
\qquad
m_{j} \;=\; a_{j}+1.
\end{equation}

The \emph{active subspace} \(V\) is the linear span of the active
coordinate directions,
\begin{equation}
V \;:=\; \mathrm{span}\{e_{i_{0}},\ldots,e_{i_{n}}\} \;\subset\; \R^{d};
\end{equation}
since \(\dal\) is insensitive to coordinates outside the active set,
all directional probes \(v\) below may be taken in \(V\).

The directional Taylor coefficient of order \(p\) at \(x\) along
\(v\in V\) is
\begin{equation}
\Tp(x;v) \;:=\; \frac{1}{p!}\,
   \frac{d^{p}}{d\tau^{p}}\,u(x+\tau v)\Big|_{\tau=0}
       \;=\; \frac{1}{p!}\,D^{p}u(x)[v,\ldots,v].
\label{eq:Tp-def}
\end{equation}

For a complex direction \(v\in V_{\C}:=V\otimes_{\R}\C\), we define
\(\Tp(x;v)\) by the complex multilinear extension of \(D^p u(x)\) in the
right-hand side of \eqref{eq:Tp-def}. If \(u\) admits a holomorphic
complexification \(u_{\C}\), this definition equals the order-\(p\)
coefficient of \(u_{\C}(x+\tau v)\). This distinction is needed because an
arbitrary \(C^p\) real function need not be defined at complex inputs.
Finally, \(\mathbb{K}[z]_{p}\), \(\mathbb{K}\in\{\R,\C\}\), denotes the
space of degree-\(p\) homogeneous polynomials in
\(z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\).

\section{Single-monomial partials as polynomial coefficient extraction}
\label{sec:coeff}
% =========================================================

\subsection{The generating polynomial}

For \(z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\in\R^{n+1}\) parametrizing the active
subspace via \(v=\sum_{j}z_{j}e_{i_{j}}\), the \emph{generating polynomial}
of \(u\) at \(x\) is
\begin{equation}
Q_{u}(z) \;:=\; \Tp\!\Big(x;\;\sum_{j=0}^{n}z_{j}e_{i_{j}}\Big),
\qquad z\in\R^{n+1}.
\label{eq:Qu-def}
\end{equation}
By construction \(Q_{u}\) is a degree-\(p\) homogeneous polynomial in
\(z\); the next lemma identifies its coefficients with the partial
derivatives of \(u\).

\begin{lemma}[Generating polynomial expansion]\label{lem:Qu-expand}
For \(u\in C^{p}\) on a neighborhood of \(x\),
\begin{equation}
Q_{u}(z) \;=\; \sum_{|\beta|=p}\frac{\partial^{\beta}u(x)}{\beta!}\,z^{\beta},
\label{eq:Qu-expand}
\end{equation}
where \(\beta\) ranges over multi-indices on the active variables and
\(\partial^{\beta}=\partial_{i_{0}}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{n}}^{\beta_{n}}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By the symmetric multilinearity of \(D^{p}u(x)\) and the multinomial
theorem,
\[
  D^{p}u(x)\Big[\textstyle\sum_{j}z_{j}e_{i_{j}},\ldots,
                \sum_{j}z_{j}e_{i_{j}}\Big]
  \;=\; \sum_{|\beta|=p}\frac{p!}{\beta!}\,z^{\beta}\,\partial^{\beta}u(x).
\]
Dividing by \(p!\) yields \eqref{eq:Qu-expand}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:coef-extract}
\(\dal u(x)=\nu!\,[z^{a}]\,Q_{u}(z)\), where
\(a=(a_0,\ldots,a_n)\) and \([z^{a}]\) extracts the coefficient of
\(z^{a}=z_0^{a_0}\cdots z_n^{a_n}\).
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Directional schedules}

A \emph{directional schedule} of length \(R\) for \(\dal\) is a finite
collection \((c_{r},v_{r})_{r=1}^{R}\subset\R\times V\) such that
\begin{equation}
\dal u(x) \;=\; \sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\,\Tp(x;v_{r}),
\qquad \forall\, u\in C^{p}_{x}.
\label{eq:dir-schedule}
\end{equation}

Because the schedule is required to hold for every \(u\in C^{p}_{x}\),
the \(p\)-jet of \(u\) at \(x\) may be prescribed arbitrarily on the
active variables: for any coefficients \(q_{\beta}\), the polynomial
\(\sum_{\beta}q_{\beta}\prod_{j}(y_{i_{j}}-x_{i_{j}})^{\beta_{j}}\)
has \(\partial^{\beta}u(x)=\beta!\,q_{\beta}\). This observation lets us
test identities coefficient by coefficient in \(\R[z]_{p}\).

The central design question of this paper is then:
\emph{What is the minimum length \(R^{\ast}\) of a directional schedule
\eqref{eq:dir-schedule}, and how is one constructed?}
Section~\ref{sec:bridge} reduces this to a classical algebraic question.

\section{Equivalence to monomial Waring decomposition}
\label{sec:bridge}
% =========================================================

\subsection{The bridge theorem}

For \(v\in V\), write \(v=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}e_{i_{j}}\) in active
coordinates. We pair this direction with the polynomial variable
\(z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\) by
\(v\!\cdot\!z=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}z_{j}\).

\begin{theorem}[Bridge]\label{thm:bridge}
Let \(R\in\N\), \(c_{r}\in\R\), \(v_{r}\in V\), \(r=1,\ldots,R\). The
following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=*]
\item (Directional schedule) \;
\(\dal u(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\Tp(x;v_{r})\)\quad
\(\forall\,u\in C^{p}_{x}\).
\item (Polynomial identity in \(\R[z]_{p}\)) \;
\(p!\,z^{a}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}(v_{r}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write \(v_{r}=(v_{r,0},\ldots,v_{r,n})\) in active coordinates. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:Qu-expand},
\[
  \Tp(x;v_{r})
  =\sum_{|\beta|=p}\frac{\partial^{\beta}u(x)}{\beta!}\,v_{r}^{\beta}.
\]
Substituting this expansion into statement (1), and using that the
order-\(p\) active jet of \(u\) at \(x\) can be prescribed arbitrarily,
shows that (1) is equivalent to the finite-dimensional coefficient system
\begingroup\renewcommand{\theHequation}{eqstar}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\,v_{r}^{\beta}
\;=\; \nu!\,\delta_{a\beta},
\qquad \forall\,\beta\in\Z_{\ge 0}^{n+1}\text{ with }|\beta|=p.
\tag{$\star$}\label{eq:star}
\end{equation}
\endgroup
On the other hand, expanding statement (2) gives
\((v_{r}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}=\sum_{|\beta|=p}\binom{p}{\beta}v_{r}^{\beta}z^{\beta}\)
and hence, after comparing coefficients of \(z^{\beta}\),
\[
\binom{p}{\beta}\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\,v_{r}^{\beta}
\;=\; p!\,\delta_{a\beta},\quad\forall\,|\beta|=p.
\]
Using \(p!/\binom{p}{\beta}=\beta!\) and
\(\beta!\,\delta_{a\beta}=\nu!\,\delta_{a\beta}\), this is
exactly \eqref{eq:star}. Thus (1) and (2) are equivalent.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Waring rank]\label{def:waring-rank}
For a homogeneous polynomial \(F\in\R[z]_{p}\), its \emph{real Waring
rank} is
\[
R_{\R}(F)\;:=\;\min\Big\{R:\;F=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}L_{r}(z)^{p},\;
c_{r}\in\R,\;L_{r}\in\R[z]_{1}\Big\}.
\]
The \emph{complex Waring rank} \(R_{\C}(F)\) is defined identically
with \(\R\) replaced by \(\C\); since \(\R\subset\C\),
\(R_{\C}(F)\le R_{\R}(F)\).
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:R-equals-rank}
The minimum length \(R^{\ast}\) of a directional schedule for \(\dal\)
equals the real Waring rank \(R_{\R}(z^{a})\) of the monomial.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Complex extension]\label{rem:complex-extension}
Using the complexified directional coefficient defined after
\eqref{eq:Tp-def}, the same coefficient comparison holds for
\(c_r\in\C\), \(v_r\in V_{\C}\), and identities in \(\C[z]_p\). The
minimum length of a complex schedule is therefore \(R_{\C}(z^{a})\).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Waring rank of a monomial}\label{ssec:waring-rank}

The closed-form rank over \(\C\) is classical. (Problem (P)/(Q) is posed
over \(\R\) by default; passing to \(\C\) can only decrease the rank, so
\(R_{\C}\le R_{\R}\).)

\begin{theorem}[Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita \cite{CCG2012}]
\label{thm:CCG}
For \(z^{a}=z_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots z_{n}^{a_{n}}\) with
\(1\le a_{0}\le\cdots\le a_{n}\),
\begin{equation}
R_{\C}(z^{a}) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}+1)
                     \;=\; \frac{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(a_{j}+1)}{a_{0}+1}.
\label{eq:CCG-rank}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The lower bound uses the complete-intersection apolar ideal
\(I_{z^{a}}=(\partial_{0}^{a_{0}+1},\ldots,\partial_{n}^{a_{n}+1})\);
Appendix~\ref{app:apolar-pairing} recalls the apolar interpretation. The
upper bound is constructive.

\begin{theorem}[Roots-of-unity directional schedule]\label{thm:rou}
Let \(m_{j}=a_{j}+1\) for \(j=1,\ldots,n\), and for each tuple
\(\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n})\) with \(\zeta_{j}^{m_{j}}=1\) define
\begin{equation}
v_{\zeta}=e_{i_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}e_{i_{j}},
\qquad
c_{\zeta}=\frac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}.
\label{eq:rou-coeff}
\end{equation}
Then \(\dal u(x)=\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}\,\Tp(x;v_{\zeta})\) for every
\(u\in C^{p}_{x}\). The schedule has length
\(\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}=R_{\C}(z^{a})\) and is therefore length-optimal
over \(\C\).
\end{theorem}

The proof, given in Appendix~\ref{app:rou-proof}, verifies condition (2)
of Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} directly via the roots-of-unity filter
\(\sum_{\zeta^{m}=1}\zeta^{k}=m\,\mathbf{1}_{m\mid k}\).

To illustrate how Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} couples the analytic side
\eqref{eq:dir-schedule} (problem~(P)) with the algebraic side
\(p!\,z^{a}=\sum_{r}c_{r}(v_{r}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\) (problem~(Q)), we
work out two examples: a low-order one for which both sides reduce to
hand-checkable identities, and a higher-order repeated-index one that
exhibits the full strength of the complex Waring construction.

\begin{example}[\(p=2\), \(a=(1,1)\), real schedule of length \(2\)]
\label{ex:u12}
For \(a=(1,1)\) we have \(n=1\), \(a_{0}=a_{1}=1\), \(m_{1}=2\),
\(U_{2}=\{+1,-1\}\). Equation~\eqref{eq:rou-coeff} produces the schedule
\begin{equation}
\big(c_{+1},v_{+1}\big)=\big(\tfrac{1}{2},\,e_{1}+e_{2}\big),
\qquad
\big(c_{-1},v_{-1}\big)=\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\,e_{1}-e_{2}\big).
\label{eq:ex-u12-schedule}
\end{equation}

\emph{Verification on the analytic side.} Expanding the directional
Taylor coefficient
\(\Tp(x;v)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(u_{11}v_{1}^{2}+2u_{12}v_{1}v_{2}+u_{22}v_{2}^{2}\bigr)\),
\[
\tfrac{1}{2}\Tp(x;e_{1}+e_{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}\Tp(x;e_{1}-e_{2})
=\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl[(u_{11}+2u_{12}+u_{22})-(u_{11}-2u_{12}+u_{22})\bigr]
=u_{12}(x).
\]

\emph{Verification on the algebraic side.} The equivalent polynomial
identity in \(\R[z]_{2}\) reads
\[
2!\,z_{0}z_{1}
\;=\;
\tfrac{1}{2}(z_{0}+z_{1})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(z_{0}-z_{1})^{2}
\;=\;
\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(z_{0}^{2}+2z_{0}z_{1}+z_{1}^{2})-(z_{0}^{2}-2z_{0}z_{1}+z_{1}^{2})\bigr]
\;=\;2\,z_{0}z_{1},
\]
matching \(p!\,z^{a}=2\,z_{0}z_{1}\). The two checks coincide via
the bridge of Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[\(p=6\), \(a=(2,2,2)\), complex schedule of length \(9\)]
\label{ex:u112233}
Consider \(u_{112233}=\partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}\partial_{3}^{2}u\),
i.e.\ \(a=(2,2,2)\). Here \(n=2\), \(a_{0}=a_{1}=a_{2}=2\),
\(m_{1}=m_{2}=3\); take \(\omega=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\) and
\(U_{3}=\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}\). Equation~\eqref{eq:rou-coeff}
produces \(R_{\C}(z^{(2,2,2)})=9\) directions: for each
\((\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in U_{3}\times U_{3}\),
\begin{equation}
v_{\zeta}\;=\;e_{1}+\zeta_{1}e_{2}+\zeta_{2}e_{3},
\qquad
c_{\zeta}\;=\;\frac{(2!)^{3}}{3\cdot 3}\,\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}
\;=\;\frac{8}{9}\,\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}.
\label{eq:ex-u112233-schedule}
\end{equation}

\emph{Analytic side.} The directional schedule reads
\begin{equation}
u_{112233}(x)
\;=\;
\frac{8}{9}\!\!\sum_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in U_{3}}\!\!
   \zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\,
   \Tp\!\bigl(x;\,e_{1}+\zeta_{1}e_{2}+\zeta_{2}e_{3}\bigr).
\label{eq:ex-u112233-P}
\end{equation}

\emph{Algebraic side.} The equivalent polynomial identity in \(\C[z]_{6}\) is
\begin{equation}
6!\,z_{0}^{2}z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}
\;=\;
\frac{8}{9}\!\!\sum_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in U_{3}}\!\!
   \zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\,(z_{0}+\zeta_{1}z_{1}+\zeta_{2}z_{2})^{6}.
\label{eq:ex-u112233-Q}
\end{equation}
That \eqref{eq:ex-u112233-Q} holds is verified by the roots-of-unity
filter (Lemma~\ref{lem:rou-filter}): expanding the right-hand side
multinomially and collecting the coefficient of \(z_{0}^{\beta_{0}}
z_{1}^{\beta_{1}}z_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\) reduces to the product
\(\prod_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{\zeta_{j}\in U_{3}}\zeta_{j}^{1+\beta_{j}}\),
which is non-zero exactly when \(\beta_{j}\equiv 2\pmod{3}\) for
\(j=1,2\); together with \(\sum_{j}\beta_{j}=6\) this forces
\(\beta=(2,2,2)\), and the leading constant
\(\tfrac{8}{9}\cdot\tfrac{6!}{2!\,2!\,2!}\cdot 9=720=6!\) gives the
right normalisation.

By comparison, the coalesced antipodally merged real polarization
schedule used in our baseline requires 13 distinct nonzero directions
for the same \(u_{112233}(x)\); the complex Waring schedule above
attains the complex Waring-rank lower bound \(R_{\C}=9\)
(Theorem~\ref{thm:CCG}).
\end{example}

\section{Taylor-mode evaluation and backend dispatch}
\label{sec:impl}
% =========================================================

\subsection{The Taylor jet for \texorpdfstring{Linear--\(\sinh\)}{Linear-sinh} MLPs}

Given a single direction \(v\), Theorem~\ref{thm:rou} reduces the problem
to evaluating \(\Tp(x;v)\). We use forward-mode Taylor propagation
\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008}, replacing each intermediate activation
\(y\in\mathbb{K}^{B}\), \(\mathbb K\in\{\R,\C\}\), by a
length-\((p+1)\) tuple \((y_{0},\ldots,y_{p})\)
representing its truncated Taylor coefficients
\(y_{k}=(1/k!)\,d^{k}y/d\tau^{k}|_{\tau=0}\).

For an MLP \(u=\phi_{L}\circ A_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}\circ A_{1}\)
with affine \(A_{\ell}\) and entrywise activations \(\phi_{\ell}\), the
jet rules are as follows. The affine layer \(y=Wx+b\) propagates each
jet order independently:
\begin{equation}
y_{0}\;=\;W x_{0}+b,
\qquad y_{k}\;=\;W x_{k}\quad (k\ge 1).
\label{eq:jet-affine}
\end{equation}
The \(\tanh\) activation, with auxiliary jet \(z=1-y^{2}\), satisfies
\begin{equation}
y_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,z_{j}\,x_{k-j},
\qquad
z_{k}\;=\;-\sum_{j=0}^{k}y_{j}\,y_{k-j},
\qquad k\ge 1.
\label{eq:jet-tanh}
\end{equation}
The \(\sinh\) activation, with auxiliary jet \(z=\cosh(x)\), satisfies
the coupled recurrence
\begin{equation}
y_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,z_{j}\,x_{k-j},
\qquad
z_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,y_{j}\,x_{k-j},
\qquad k\ge 1.
\label{eq:jet-sinh}
\end{equation}
The \(\sinh/\cosh\) recurrence has the same coupled form as the
\(\sin/\cos\) recurrence, but the \(\cosh\) update has no sign flip. These
recurrences are algebraic identities and can be
evaluated on complex tensors. We use \(\sinh\) for complex-parameter
networks because \(\sinh\) and \(\cosh\) are entire. The implementation also
contains \(\sin\) and sigmoid recurrences for the benchmark architectures.
Although the local jet of \(\tanh\) is well defined away from a pole,
\(\tanh\) is meromorphic rather than entire.

Each Taylor coefficient \(y_{k}\) is a regular tensor, and the jet
propagation does not nest input-gradient graphs. It stores \(p+1\)
coefficient tensors per activation. We additionally use a
custom \texttt{torch.autograd.Function} for each activation jet, with
the closed-form backward
\begin{equation}
g_{x}^{(m)}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{p-m}\overline{q_{j}}\,g_{y}^{(m+j)},
\qquad q(\tau)\;=\;\phi'(x(\tau)),
\label{eq:jet-vjp}
\end{equation}
where \(q_j\) is the \(j\)th Taylor coefficient of \(q\). This
vector--Jacobian product avoids reverse differentiation through the
intra-jet recurrence.

\subsection{Backends}\label{ssec:backends}

We compare three implemented deterministic backends for evaluating one
fixed \(\dal u(x)\). All three are differentiable in the network
parameters \(\theta\), so each can be used directly as the residual term
of a PINN loss. The implementation also provides an \texttt{auto}
dispatch option, but it is a selection rule between B2 and B3 rather
than a separate mathematical backend.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(B1) Direct nested AD.}
The reference baseline performs \(p\) successive calls to PyTorch's
\texttt{autograd.grad}, each with retained computation graph. This
induces a reverse-mode autograd graph of depth \(O(pL)\) for a
depth-\(L\) network.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(B2) Real polarization with Taylor jet.}
The classical antipodally merged polarization identity is
\begin{equation}
\dal u(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{2^{p}}\sum_{\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{p}}
   \Big(\textstyle\prod_{r=1}^{p}\epsilon_{r}\Big)\,
   \Tp\!\Big(x;\,\textstyle\sum_{r=1}^{p}\epsilon_{r}\,e_{i_{r}}\Big).
\label{eq:polarization}
\end{equation}
After antipodal merging this yields at most \(2^{p-1}\) real directions.
The directional Taylor coefficients \(\Tp(x;\,\cdot\,)\) are evaluated
by the jet propagation of Section~\ref{sec:impl}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(B3) Complex Waring with Taylor jet.}
The roots-of-unity construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:rou} produces
\(R_{\C}(z^{a})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}+1)\) directions in
\(\C^{n+1}\); for several repeated-index patterns this is smaller than
the coalesced polarization count of (B2). For real-parameter networks,
the complex directions induce differentiable casts of the linear weights
via \(\mathtt{Tensor.to}\); for complex-parameter networks no cast is
required and the network
itself acts on the complex directions natively.

\subsection{The end-to-end algorithm}\label{ssec:algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:waring-jet} summarizes the complete pipeline of
backend (B3): given a smooth model \(u\) (an MLP whose primitives are
covered by the jet rules \eqref{eq:jet-affine}--\eqref{eq:jet-sinh}), a
point \(x\), and a multi-index \(\nu\) of order \(p\), it returns
the value \(\dal u(x)\) without nested input differentiation. The
implementation batches all schedule directions in one jet pass.
Backend (B2) follows the same overall structure with the
Waring schedule (lines~3--7) replaced by the polarization schedule
\eqref{eq:polarization}.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Single-monomial \(\dal u(x)\) using a complex Waring schedule and
a batched Taylor jet.}
\label{alg:waring-jet}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE smooth scalar field \(u\); point \(x\in\R^{d}\);
         multi-index \(\nu\) of order \(p\)
\ENSURE  the value \(\dal u(x)\)
\STATE relabel the active coordinates of \(\nu\) as
       \(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}\) with exponents
       \(1\le a_{0}\le\cdots\le a_{n}\)
\STATE \(m_{j}\leftarrow a_{j}+1\) for \(j=1,\ldots,n\); set
       \(\mathcal{I}\leftarrow U_{m_{1}}\!\times\cdots\times U_{m_{n}}\)
\STATE initialise schedule \(\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\emptyset\)
\FORALL{\(\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n})\in\mathcal{I}\)}
   \STATE \(v_{\zeta}\leftarrow e_{i_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}\,e_{i_{j}}\)
   \STATE \(c_{\zeta}\leftarrow
          \dfrac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}\)
   \STATE \(\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{S}\cup\{(c_{\zeta},v_{\zeta})\}\)
\ENDFOR
\STATE stack the directions into
       \(V_{\mathcal S}\in\C^{|\mathcal S|\times d}\)
\STATE form the batched input jet
       \(\mathbf{J}_{0}\leftarrow(x\text{ replicated},V_{\mathcal S},0,\ldots,0)\)
\STATE \(\mathbf{J}_{L}\leftarrow\) propagate \(\mathbf{J}_{0}\) through \(u\)
       using \eqref{eq:jet-affine}--\eqref{eq:jet-sinh}
\STATE \(t\leftarrow[\mathbf{J}_{L}]_{p}\)
       \quad{\small$\triangleright$ one order-\(p\) coefficient per direction}
\RETURN \(\sum_{r=1}^{|\mathcal S|}c_r t_r\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:correctness}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} together with Theorem~\ref{thm:rou},
Algorithm~\ref{alg:waring-jet} returns \(\dal u(x)\) algebraically exactly;
floating-point evaluation introduces round-off. The batch contains
\(|\mathcal{S}|=R_{\C}(z^{a})\) directions, the minimum possible for a
complex pure-power formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:CCG}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Cost and numerical considerations}

For a fixed direction, the Taylor jet stores \(p+1\) coefficient tensors
per activation and applies each linear map to the stacked coefficients;
there is no chain of nested reverse-mode differentiation through the
input coordinates. The total value-evaluation cost of B3 is therefore
proportional to \(R_{\C}(z^{a})\) batched directional jets, while
B2 scales with the coalesced polarization count \(R_{\mathrm{pol}}\).
The rank reduction is an algebraic cost reduction; the realized
wall-clock time also depends on complex arithmetic, batching,
activation kernels, and whether parameter gradients are required.

The schedule coefficients are roots of unity multiplied by
\(\nu!/R_{\C}\), so coefficient magnitudes are explicit and do not
come from solving an ill-conditioned linear system. The method has no
finite-difference step size and no Monte Carlo variance. Remaining
errors are ordinary floating-point round-off from Taylor recurrences,
complex arithmetic, and the final summation over directions.

\section{Implementation verification}
\label{sec:experiments}
% =========================================================

We verify separately the algebraic schedule, derivative values, and
parameter gradients. All checks use double precision. First, the
roots-of-unity coefficient filter is evaluated against every degree-four
monomial in five variables for the active-exponent pattern \((2,1,1)\);
the maximum admissible absolute coefficient error is \(10^{-12}\). The
direction counts for all patterns in Table~\ref{tab:patterns} are also checked
against the closed-form rank and the coalesced polarization construction.

Second, B2 and B3 are compared with B1 on real-parameter networks using
\(\tanh\), sigmoid, \(\sin\), and \(\sinh\) activations. The tests cover the
patterns \((2,2)\), \((1,1,1)\), and \((3)\). Values must agree with nested
automatic differentiation to relative tolerance \(2\times10^{-10}\) and
absolute tolerance \(2\times10^{-11}\); the imaginary remainder for a real
target is bounded by \(2\times10^{-11}\).

Third, gradients of a squared derivative loss with respect to every network
parameter are compared between the jet backends and nested differentiation.
The tolerance is \(2\times10^{-9}\) relative and \(2\times10^{-11}\)
absolute for both real- and complex-parameter networks. Additional analytic
checks cover orders \(1,2,3,4,\) and \(6\) for a scalar complex
\(\sinh\) network. These tests establish implementation consistency, but they
do not convert a direction-count reduction into a hardware-independent timing
claim. The application study below therefore reports measured throughput
together with accuracy.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Direction count and computational cost}

The complex schedule is most compact for repeated, balanced active exponents,
including \((2,2)\), \((2,2,2)\), \((3,3)\), and \((4,2)\). For
\((2,2,2)\), for example, it uses 9 directions rather than the 13 produced by
coalesced real polarization. This reduction controls the batch size of the
directional jet, but not every component of execution time. Complex arithmetic,
kernel launch overhead, memory traffic, and the backward pass can dominate for
small networks or small batches. The rank theorem should therefore be read as
an exact algebraic complexity result, not as a hardware-independent speedup.

\subsection{Interpretation of the PINN study}

Under the frozen \texttt{jsc\_v2} protocol, Complex \(\sinh\) has the lowest
mean error in all 12 benchmark settings. The strongest separation occurs in
the two-dimensional fourth- and sixth-order polyharmonic problems and in the
complex-valued Maxwell problem. The three-dimensional sixth-order problem is a
necessary counterweight: all methods remain near unit relative error, and the
difference between Complex \(\sinh\) and SIREN is practically negligible.

The comparison does not isolate one causal mechanism. A complex model changes
the value representation, initialization, parameter count, and throughput at
the same time. Literal width \(H=128\) gives the scalar Complex \(\sinh\)
network roughly twice as many real degrees of freedom as SIREN, whereas
MscaleDNN-2-sin has more degrees of freedom but completes fewer steps. The loss
weights and optimizer are shared rather than tuned separately for every
architecture. The reported result is therefore a controlled comparison of
complete methods under one compute protocol, not proof that complex parameters
are intrinsically superior.

\subsection{Operator scope and stochastic alternatives}

Square-free patterns attain \(R_{\C}=2^{p-1}\), so complex Waring gives no
direction-count reduction. Operator sums can be expanded and evaluated
monomial by monomial, as in the polyharmonic experiment, but this procedure is
not jointly rank-optimal for the full sum. Trace-collapsing approaches
\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian,Gao2023folx} can be preferable for Laplacian-only
workloads.

The Stochastic Taylor Derivative Estimator \cite{Shi2024stde} solves a
strictly broader problem -- arbitrary contraction of the order-\(k\)
derivative tensor -- via stochastic sparse jets driven by integer
partitions. For one fixed multi-index \(\nu\), our schedule is algebraically
exact, whereas STDE is unbiased with variance controlled by its sampling
budget. The approaches are complementary: stochastic estimation addresses
large contractions, while Waring provides a minimal deterministic formula for
one monomial derivative.

% =========================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =========================================================

We have shown that the construction of a directional schedule for one
mixed partial derivative \(\dal u\) of order \(p\) is exactly the problem
of a Waring decomposition of the monomial \(z^{a}\). The classical
Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita rank theorem and its roots-of-unity
construction yield an explicit, rank-optimal directional schedule over
\(\C\). Combined with batched Taylor-mode propagation and a custom
vector--Jacobian product, it gives an algebraically exact,
parameter-differentiable backend for high-order neural surrogates.

The method is at its most useful for repeated-index multi-indices, where
the complex Waring rank is materially smaller than the coalesced real
polarization count. For square-free patterns it offers no rank advantage;
for operator sums it is applied term by term without a global optimality
claim. The same entire-on-\(\C\) implementation supports complex-parameter
\(\sinh\) PINNs. In the validated five-seed application study, that complete
method gives the lowest mean error on all 12 tested settings, but all methods
saturate on the hardest three-dimensional sixth-order case. These results
support the method as a useful deterministic component for repeated
high-order derivatives and complex-valued PDE surrogates, while preserving the
need for operator-specific methods in broader contractions.

\section{Proofs of directional schedule identities}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Roots-of-unity filter}\label{app:rou-filter}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rou-filter}
For any positive integer \(m\) and integer \(k\),
\(\sum_{\zeta:\,\zeta^{m}=1}\zeta^{k}=m\,\mathbf{1}_{\{m\mid k\}}\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If \(m\mid k\) every term is \(1\); otherwise the sum is a non-trivial
geometric series in the primitive \(m\)-th root of unity, which sums to
zero.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rou}}\label{app:rou-proof}
We verify condition (2) of Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}: namely that the
schedule \eqref{eq:rou-coeff} satisfies
\(p!\,z^{a}=\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}(v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\) in
\(\C[z]_{p}\).

Expanding \((v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\) by the multinomial theorem and
collecting the coefficient of \(z^{\beta}\) (with \(|\beta|=p\)):
\[
[z^{\beta}]\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}(v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}
=\frac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}}\binom{p}{\beta}
 \prod_{j=1}^{n}\!\Big(\sum_{\zeta_{j}^{m_{j}}=1}\zeta_{j}^{1+\beta_{j}}\Big).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:rou-filter}, each inner sum equals \(m_{j}\) iff
\(m_{j}\mid 1+\beta_{j}\), i.e.\ \(\beta_{j}\equiv a_{j}\pmod{m_{j}}\),
and equals zero otherwise.

A short counting argument shows that the unique feasible joint assignment
is \(\beta=a\). Indeed, the congruence forces
\(\beta_{j}=a_{j}+t_{j}(a_{j}+1)\) for \(j\ge 1\) with
\(t_{j}\ge 0\). If some \(t_{j}>0\), then
\[
\beta_{0}
=a_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}(a_{j}+1)
<0,
\]
because \(a_{j}\ge a_{0}\), impossible. Hence all \(t_{j}=0\) and
\(\beta=a\). With \(\beta=a\) all the \(m_{j}\) factors cancel
and the leading constant becomes
\(\nu!\binom{p}{a}=p!\), giving
\([z^{a}]\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}(v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}=p!\) and
all other coefficients vanish. \qed

\subsection{Apolar pairing alternative}\label{app:apolar-pairing}
The Waring rank \(R_{\C}(F)\) of a homogeneous polynomial \(F\in\C[z]_{p}\)
admits the apolar characterization
\(R_{\C}(F)=\min\{\deg X:X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}\text{ a finite reduced
scheme apolar to }F\}\), where \(X\) is apolar to \(F\) iff
\(I_{X}\subset F^{\perp}\) and \(F^{\perp}\) is the apolar (Macaulay
inverse) ideal of \(F\). For \(F=z^{a}\),
\(F^{\perp}=(\partial_{0}^{a_{0}+1},\ldots,\partial_{n}^{a_{n}+1})\),
which is a complete intersection. The Hilbert function of the quotient
\(\C[\partial]/F^{\perp}\) gives the lower bound matching
\eqref{eq:CCG-rank}; details in \cite{IK1999,Landsberg2012}.
