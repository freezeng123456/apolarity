%% ========== Abstract ==========
\begin{abstract}
  Computing high-order derivatives with neural networks is often
  computationally and memory intensive, primarily due to the huge computational
  graphs caused by automatic differentiation. In this work, we decompose the
  derivative into directional Taylor-mode automatic differentiation. We
  establish a fundamental connection between derivative extraction and
  polynomial algebra, proving that the minimal number of directional
  evaluations required to recover a target partial derivative equals the
  Waring rank of its corresponding monomial. We present a roots-of-unity
  formula that strictly attains this theoretical lower bound. Experiments show
  that our method significantly outperforms traditional automatic
  differentiation in speed and memory efficiency.
\end{abstract}

Taylor-mode automatic differentiation,
Waring decomposition,
apolarity
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec_intro}

High-order derivatives of a neural network are usually computed by applying
automatic differentiation repeatedly~\cite{Griewank2008,Baydin2018,Paszke2019}.
Each extra derivative is another backpropagation through a larger
computational graph. Time and memory therefore grow with the order, and with
how many coordinates are differentiated. Polyharmonic and plate
operators~\cite{Gazzola2010,Ciarlet1997}, Helmholtz
problems~\cite{Ihlenburg1998}, and Maxwell systems~\cite{Monk2003} are built
from those mixed partials, and the same values are needed at many points.

Finite differences replace each extra derivative by nearby evaluations of
the same network~\cite{Fornberg1988}. Some methods mix automatic
differentiation with finite differences~\cite{Chiu2022CAN}. Sampling methods
estimate the derivative instead of forming the full graph. Hutchinson's
estimator~\cite{Hutchinson1989}, applied through Hessian-vector
products~\cite{Pearlmutter1994}, estimates traces of high-order
operators~\cite{Hu2024hte}. Stein's identity and Gaussian
smoothing~\cite{Stein1981,He2023} estimate derivatives from function values,
and the bias and variance of that smoothing have been
quantified~\cite{Hu2025bias}. A random subset of coordinates can be taken at
each step~\cite{Hu2023sdgd}. The stochastic Taylor derivative estimator
already uses Taylor-mode automatic differentiation to estimate a contraction
of the derivative tensor~\cite{Shi2024stde}. Griewank, Utke, and Walther
recover every derivative of a given order from a family of univariate Taylor
series~\cite{GriewankUtkeWalther2000}. A structured recurrence evaluates one
differential operator exactly~\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian}.

These methods do different things. Repeated automatic differentiation is
exact, but each extra derivative enlarges the graph. Finite differences
introduce a step size. Sampling methods, including the stochastic Taylor
derivative estimator, introduce variance. Recovering every derivative of a
given order is exact, but the cost is set by the whole tensor, not by one
mixed partial. After Taylor-mode automatic
differentiation~\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008} has produced directional
derivatives, one still has to choose how many directions to use for a
single mixed partial.

We determine the least such number. One pass along a direction returns the
directional Taylor coefficient of that order,
\begin{equation}\label{eq_intro_tp}
    \Tp(\bx; \bv) = \frac{1}{p!}\,\frac{d^{p}}{d\tau^{p}}\,u(\bx + \tau \bv)\Big|_{\tau=0}.
\end{equation}
A linear combination of these coefficients recovers one mixed partial of
the same order,
\begin{equation}\label{eq_intro_recon}
    \dal u(\bx) = \sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r}\, \Tp(\bx; \bv_{r}).
\end{equation}
As a function of the direction, the coefficient~\eqref{eq_intro_tp} is a
homogeneous polynomial of degree $p$. The mixed partial is one monomial
coefficient of that polynomial. The shortest formula of the
form~\eqref{eq_intro_recon} is therefore a Waring decomposition of the
corresponding monomial~\cite{Landsberg2012}. Over the complex numbers that
length is known in closed form~\cite{CCG2012}, and roots of unity attain
it. Appendix~\ref{sec_schedules} recalls the argument behind the lower
bound~\cite{IK1999}. We evaluate the combination in one batch and
differentiate it with respect to the network parameters.

We compare this formula with repeated automatic differentiation on the same
network, the same residual, and the same optimizer. The values and the
parameter gradients match in double precision. Time and memory of one
evaluation fall as the order grows. Section~\ref{sec_experiments} times
both methods on manufactured collocation residuals of the operators
above~\cite{Lagaris1998,Raissi2019,Sirignano2018,Karniadakis2021,Roache2002}.
The optimizer is Adam with a cosine learning-rate
schedule~\cite{Kingma2015,Loshchilov2017}, on a complex-parameter
network~\cite{Trabelsi2018ComplexNN}. The model, the loss, and the optimizer
are held fixed between the two methods.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec_prelim}
states the evaluation problem and recalls Taylor-mode automatic
differentiation. Section~\ref{sec_formulation} proves that the shortest
directional formula is a monomial Waring decomposition and records the
algorithm. Section~\ref{sec_experiments} compares the implementation with
repeated automatic differentiation. Section~\ref{sec_concl} concludes. The
roots-of-unity formula, the schedules used in the experiments, and the
argument for the rank bound are collected in Appendix~\ref{sec_schedules}.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec_prelim}

\subsection{The evaluation problem}\label{sec_problem}

Let $u_{\theta} \colon \R^{d} \to \K$ with $\K \in \{\R, \C\}$ be smooth. Let
$\nu = (\nu_{1}, \ldots, \nu_{d}) \in \Z_{\ge 0}^{d}$ be a multi-index of order
$p = \abs{\nu}$, and write $\dal := \partial_{1}^{\nu_{1}} \cdots
\partial_{d}^{\nu_{d}}$. We evaluate $\dal u_{\theta}$ at a point, exactly, and
we differentiate the value with respect to $\theta$. The same derivative is
required at many points on every step, so the cost that matters is one
evaluation, amortized over a batch.

A differential operator of order $p$ is a linear combination of monomials
$\partial^{\alpha}$. Section~\ref{sec_experiments} times the method on
repeated evaluations of such an operator. Let $\Omega \subset \R^{d}$ be a
bounded domain with boundary $\Gamma := \partial\Omega$, and consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq_pde}
    \mathcal{P} u = f \quad\text{in } \Omega, \quad \mathcal{B} u = g \quad\text{on } \Gamma,
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{P}$ of order $p$ and $\mathcal{B}$ the boundary conditions.
Given interior points $(\bx_{r,i})_{i=1}^{n_r} \subset \Omega$ and boundary
points $(\bx_{b,i})_{i=1}^{n_b} \subset \Gamma$, a collocation
fit~\cite{Raissi2019,Karniadakis2021} of \eqref{eq_pde} minimizes
\begin{equation}\label{eq_pinn_loss}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) := \frac{\lambda_r}{n_r} \sum_{i=1}^{n_r} \abs{\mathcal{P} u_{\theta}(\bx_{r,i}) - f(\bx_{r,i})}^{2} + \frac{\lambda_b}{n_b} \sum_{i=1}^{n_b} \abs{\mathcal{B} u_{\theta}(\bx_{b,i}) - g(\bx_{b,i})}^{2}.
\end{equation}
Expanding $\mathcal{P}$ into monomials turns one residual evaluation in
\eqref{eq_pinn_loss} into several derivatives $\partial^{\alpha}u_{\theta}$ of
the same function on the same batch. The operator $\Delta^{m}$ expands into
$\binom{d+m-1}{m}$ distinct monomials: the pure terms $\partial_{x_{i}}^{2m}$
and, for $m \ge 2$, mixed terms such as
$\partial_{x_{i}}^{2k}\partial_{x_{j}}^{2m-2k}$.

\subsection{Taylor-mode evaluation of directional derivatives}\label{sec_taylor}

Let $u \colon \R^{d} \to \K$ with $\K \in \{\R, \C\}$ be sufficiently smooth
near a fixed point $\bx$. For a direction $\bv \in \R^{d}$, the directional
Taylor coefficient of order $p$ at $\bx$ along $\bv$, already written as
\eqref{eq_intro_tp}, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq_tp}
    \Tp(\bx; \bv) := \frac{1}{p!}\,\frac{d^{p}}{d\tau^{p}}\,u(\bx + \tau \bv)\Big|_{\tau=0} = \frac{1}{p!}\,D^{p}u(\bx)[\bv, \ldots, \bv].
\end{equation}
Taylor-mode automatic differentiation evaluates \eqref{eq_tp} in one forward
pass~\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008}. Each intermediate activation $y \in
\K^{B}$ over a batch of $B$ points, with $\K \in \{\R, \C\}$, is replaced by the
length-$(p+1)$ tuple $(y_{0}, \ldots, y_{p})$ of its truncated Taylor
coefficients
\begin{equation}\label{eq_jet_coeff}
    y_{k} = \frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{d^{k}y}{d\tau^{k}}\Big|_{\tau=0}.
\end{equation}
For a multilayer perceptron
$u = \phi_{L} \circ A_{L} \circ \cdots \circ \phi_{1} \circ A_{1}$
with affine maps $A_{\ell}$ and entrywise activations $\phi_{\ell}$, an affine
layer $y = Wx + b$ propagates each order independently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq_jet_affine}
    y_{0} = W x_{0} + b, \quad y_{k} = W x_{k} \quad (k \ge 1),
\end{equation}
and each nonlinearity is propagated together with one auxiliary jet that closes
its derivative recurrence. For $\tanh$ the auxiliary jet is $z = 1 - y^{2}$,
initialized by $y_{0} = \tanh(x_{0})$ and $z_{0} = 1 - y_{0}^{2}$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq_jet_tanh}
    y_{k} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,z_{j}\,x_{k-j}, \quad z_{k} = -\sum_{j=0}^{k} y_{j}\,y_{k-j}, \quad k \ge 1.
\end{equation}
For $\sinh$ the auxiliary jet is $z = \cosh(x)$, initialized by $y_{0} =
\sinh(x_{0})$ and $z_{0} = \cosh(x_{0})$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq_jet_sinh}
    y_{k} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,z_{j}\,x_{k-j}, \quad z_{k} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,y_{j}\,x_{k-j}, \quad k \ge 1.
\end{equation}
The recurrence \eqref{eq_jet_sinh} has the same coupled form as the one for
$\sin$ and $\cos$, except that the $\cosh$ update carries no sign flip. The
implementation also contains the recurrences for $\sin$ and for the logistic
sigmoid, which the verification tests of Section~\ref{sec_experiments} use.
Every coefficient $y_{k}$ is an ordinary tensor, and the propagation stores
$p+1$ coefficient tensors per activation without nesting input-gradient graphs.

\section{Rank-optimal directional schedules}\label{sec_formulation}

The argument of this section is short. Its four steps are as follows. First, the
directional Taylor coefficients of order $p$, read as a function of the
direction rather than of the point, are the coefficients of a homogeneous
polynomial of degree $p$, and the target derivative is one of them. Second, a
formula that reconstructs the derivative from finitely many directional
evaluations is therefore a linear identity among such coefficients, which
reduces the design problem to a finite system of moment conditions. Third,
pairing each direction with the polynomial variable turns that system into a
single polynomial identity: the monomial belonging to the target derivative,
written as a linear combination of $p$th powers of linear forms. Fourth, the
least number of terms in such a combination is by definition the Waring rank of
that monomial, a quantity known in closed form, and one set of minimizing linear
forms consists of roots of unity. The four steps together replace a search for a
reconstruction formula by a rank computation, and because the outcome is a
minimum, it certifies that no shorter linear combination of those coefficients
exists.

We introduce some notation used throughout this section. Let $\N_{0} := \{0, 1,
2, \ldots\}$, and let $\C^{d}$ be the $d$-dimensional complex space. The
\emph{active coordinates} of $\nu$ are the indices $k$ with $\nu_{k} > 0$; we
relabel them as $i_{0}, \ldots, i_{n}$ and set $a_{j} := \nu_{i_{j}}$, ordered
so that $1 \le a_{0} \le \cdots \le a_{n}$, with ties broken by the original
coordinate index. The ordering singles out a coordinate $i_{0}$ of least
exponent, which is the coordinate the rank formula treats separately. We write
$a := (a_{0}, \ldots, a_{n})$, and we record for later use that
\begin{equation}\label{eq_active}
    p = \sum_{j=0}^{n} a_{j}, \quad \nu! = \prod_{k=1}^{d}\nu_{k}! = \prod_{j=0}^{n} a_{j}!, \quad m_{j} := a_{j} + 1.
\end{equation}
The \emph{active subspace} is $V := \spanop\{e_{i_{0}}, \ldots, e_{i_{n}}\}
\subset \R^{d}$, and $V_{\C} := V \otimes_{\R} \C$ is its complexification.
Since $\dal$ is insensitive to coordinates outside the active set, all
directional probes below may be taken in $V$ or in $V_{\C}$. For $\K \in \{\R,
\C\}$, the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree $p$ in $\bz = (z_{0},
\ldots, z_{n})$ is denoted $\K[\bz]_{p}$, and $[\bz^{a}]F$ denotes the
coefficient of $\bz^{a} = z_{0}^{a_{0}} \cdots z_{n}^{a_{n}}$ in $F$. For $m \ge
1$, let $U_{m} := \{\zeta \in \C : \zeta^{m} = 1\}$, and let $\mathbf{1}_{A}$ be
the indicator of a condition $A$.

The minimizing schedule of this section is in general complex, so we fix the
meaning of a complex direction once. For $\bv \in V_{\C}$ we define
$\Tp(\bx; \bv)$ by the complex multilinear extension of $D^{p}u(\bx)$ on the
right-hand side of \eqref{eq_tp}.
When $u$ admits a holomorphic extension to a neighborhood of $\bx$ in $\C^{d}$,
this definition agrees with the order-$p$ coefficient of $\tau \mapsto u(\bx +
\tau \bv)$, and the recurrences \eqref{eq_jet_affine}--\eqref{eq_jet_sinh}
evaluate it on complex tensors. When the directions are complex, the extension
is guaranteed by entire activations such as $\sinh(z) = (e^{z}-e^{-z})/2$; see
Remark~\ref{rmk_entire}. The distinction matters because an arbitrary $C^{p}$
function on $\R^{d}$ need not be defined at complex arguments.

\subsection{Basic expansion}\label{sec_expansion}

Let $\bz \in \C^{n+1}$ parametrize the active subspace through $\bv(\bz) :=
\sum_{j=0}^{n} z_{j}e_{i_{j}}$, and define the \emph{generating polynomial} of
$u$ at $\bx$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq_qu}
    Q_{u}(\bz) := \Tp\bigl(\bx; \bv(\bz)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The symmetric $p$-linearity of $D^{p}u(\bx)$ and the multinomial theorem give
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    D^{p}u(\bx)\bigl[\bv(\bz), \ldots, \bv(\bz)\bigr] &= \sum_{\abs{\beta}=p} \binom{p}{\beta} \bz^{\beta}\,\partial^{\beta}u(\bx) \\
    &= \sum_{\abs{\beta}=p} \frac{p!}{\beta!}\,\bz^{\beta}\,\partial^{\beta}u(\bx),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where $\beta$ ranges over multi-indices on the active variables and
$\partial^{\beta} := \partial_{i_{0}}^{\beta_{0}} \cdots
\partial_{i_{n}}^{\beta_{n}}$. Dividing by $p!$ and comparing with \eqref{eq_tp}
and \eqref{eq_qu} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq_qu_expand}
    Q_{u}(\bz) = \sum_{\abs{\beta}=p} \frac{\partial^{\beta}u(\bx)}{\beta!}\,\bz^{\beta},
\end{equation}
so $Q_{u}$ is homogeneous of degree $p$ and its coefficients are the order-$p$
derivatives of $u$ at $\bx$. Reading off the coefficient of $\bz^{a}$ in
\eqref{eq_qu_expand} and using $a! = \nu!$ from \eqref{eq_active} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq_extract}
    \dal u(\bx) = \nu!\,[\bz^{a}]\,Q_{u}(\bz).
\end{equation}
A single order-$p$ derivative is therefore one coefficient of a homogeneous
polynomial of degree $p$.

The evaluations available to us are values of $Q_{u}$ at individual directions,
so we ask for the shortest linear combination of such values that realizes the
functional \eqref{eq_extract}. A \emph{directional schedule} of length $R$ for
$\dal$ over $\K$ is a finite collection $(c_{r}, \bv_{r})_{r=1}^{R} \subset \K
\times V_{\K}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq_schedule}
    \dal u(\bx) = \sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r}\,\Tp(\bx; \bv_{r})
\end{equation}
for every $u$ that is $C^{p}$ on a neighborhood of $\bx$, and, when $\K = \C$,
holomorphic there. Because \eqref{eq_schedule} is required for all such $u$, the
order-$p$ derivatives $(\partial^{\beta}u(\bx))_{\abs{\beta}=p}$ may be
prescribed independently: for any coefficients $q_{\beta}$, the polynomial
$\sum_{\beta} q_{\beta} \prod_{j}(y_{i_{j}} - x_{i_{j}})^{\beta_{j}}$ has
$\partial^{\beta}u(\bx) = \beta!\,q_{\beta}$. Substituting \eqref{eq_qu_expand}
into the right-hand side of \eqref{eq_schedule} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq_schedule_sum}
    \sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r}\,\Tp(\bx; \bv_{r}) = \sum_{\abs{\beta}=p} \frac{\partial^{\beta}u(\bx)}{\beta!} \Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r} \bv_{r}^{\beta}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
and comparing \eqref{eq_schedule_sum} with the target $\dal u(\bx) =
\partial^{a}u(\bx)$ coefficient by coefficient shows that \eqref{eq_schedule}
holds for every admissible $u$ if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq_moment}
    \sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r} \bv_{r}^{\beta} = \nu!\,\delta_{a\beta}, \quad \abs{\beta} = p,
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{a\beta} := 1$ if $\beta = a$ and $\delta_{a\beta} := 0$
otherwise. The design problem is thus to solve the moment conditions
\eqref{eq_moment} with as few directions as possible.

Two features of \eqref{eq_moment} decide how short a schedule can be. The
conditions number $\binom{p+n}{n}$, so their count is governed by how many
coordinates the derivative touches and not by the order alone: a derivative of
order six on three coordinates imposes far fewer conditions than one of order
six on six coordinates. They are also nonlinear in the unknowns, since each
direction enters through the monomials $\bv_{r}^{\beta}$, which is why the
minimum is not read off by counting equations and unknowns.
Section~\ref{sec_main} resolves both by recognizing \eqref{eq_moment} as a
polynomial identity whose shortest solution is classical.

\subsection{Minimal schedules and monomial Waring rank}\label{sec_main}

Pair a direction $\bv \in V_{\C}$, written $\bv = \sum_{j=0}^{n} v_{j}e_{i_{j}}$
in active coordinates, with the polynomial variable $\bz$ through $\bv \cdot \bz
:= \sum_{j=0}^{n} v_{j}z_{j}$. Expanding the $p$th power of this pairing by the
multinomial theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq_power_expand}
    \sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r} (\bv_{r} \cdot \bz)^{p} = \sum_{\abs{\beta}=p} \binom{p}{\beta} \Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r} \bv_{r}^{\beta}\Bigr) \bz^{\beta},
\end{equation}
whose coefficients are the very quantities constrained by \eqref{eq_moment}. The
moment conditions are therefore a polynomial identity in disguise, and the
shortest schedule is the shortest decomposition of a monomial into pure powers
of linear forms. That notion has a classical name.

\begin{definition}[Waring rank]\label{def_waring}
    Let $\K \in \{\R, \C\}$ and let $F \in \K[\bz]_{p}$ be homogeneous of degree
    $p$. The \emph{Waring rank} $R_{\K}(F)$ is the least $R$ for which there exist
    $c_{r} \in \K$ and linear forms $L_{r} \in \K[\bz]_{1}$ with $F = \sum_{r=1}^{R}
    c_{r} L_{r}(\bz)^{p}$.
\end{definition}

Since $\R \subset \C$, every real decomposition is a complex one, so $R_{\C}(F)
\le R_{\R}(F)$, and enlarging the field can only shorten a schedule. The
following theorem is the main result of the paper. Its three parts identify the
design problem with a Waring decomposition, evaluate the resulting minimum, and
exhibit a schedule of that length in closed form.

\begin{theorem}[Rank-optimal directional schedule]\label{thm_main}
    Let $\nu$ be a multi-index of order $p \ge 1$ with active exponents $1 \le
    a_{0} \le \cdots \le a_{n}$ and $m_{j} := a_{j}+1$ as in \eqref{eq_active},
    and let $\K \in \{\R, \C\}$.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
        \item A collection $(c_{r}, \bv_{r})_{r=1}^{R} \subset \K \times V_{\K}$ is a directional schedule for $\dal$ if and only if
        \begin{equation}\label{eq_bridge}
            p!\,\bz^{a} = \sum_{r=1}^{R} c_{r} (\bv_{r} \cdot \bz)^{p} \quad\text{in } \K[\bz]_{p}.
        \end{equation}
        \item The minimum length of a directional schedule for $\dal$ over $\K$ equals $R_{\K}(\bz^{a})$, and over $\C$ this minimum is
        \begin{equation}\label{eq_rank}
            R_{\C}(\bz^{a}) = \prod_{j=1}^{n} m_{j} = \frac{\prod_{j=0}^{n} m_{j}}{m_{0}}.
        \end{equation}
        \item For $\zeta = (\zeta_{1}, \ldots, \zeta_{n}) \in U_{m_{1}} \times \cdots \times U_{m_{n}}$, put
        \begin{equation}\label{eq_rou}
            \bv_{\zeta} := e_{i_{0}} + \sum_{j=1}^{n} \zeta_{j} e_{i_{j}}, \quad c_{\zeta} := \frac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n} m_{j}} \prod_{j=1}^{n} \zeta_{j}.
        \end{equation}
        Then $(c_{\zeta}, \bv_{\zeta})_{\zeta}$ is a directional schedule for
        $\dal$ of length $\prod_{j=1}^{n} m_{j}$, and by part~(ii) no complex
        schedule is shorter.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    \subsubsection*{(i) The moment conditions are a polynomial identity}

    By \eqref{eq_moment}, the collection is a directional schedule if and only
    if $\sum_{r} c_{r}\bv_{r}^{\beta} = \nu!\,\delta_{a\beta}$ for every $\beta$
    with $\abs{\beta} = p$. By \eqref{eq_power_expand}, the identity
    \eqref{eq_bridge} holds if and only if $\binom{p}{\beta}\sum_{r}
    c_{r}\bv_{r}^{\beta} = p!\,\delta_{a\beta}$ for every such $\beta$. Dividing
    the second family of conditions by $\binom{p}{\beta}$ turns it into
    $\sum_{r} c_{r}\bv_{r}^{\beta} = \beta!\,\delta_{a\beta}$, and
    $\beta!\,\delta_{a\beta} = \nu!\,\delta_{a\beta}$ by \eqref{eq_active}. The
    two families coincide, which proves part~(i).

    \subsubsection*{(ii) The minimum length is a Waring rank}

    Absorbing the nonzero constant $p!$ into the coefficients $c_{r}$ shows that
    a decomposition of $p!\,\bz^{a}$ into $R$ pure $p$th powers exists if and
    only if one of $\bz^{a}$ does, so $R_{\K}(p!\,\bz^{a}) = R_{\K}(\bz^{a})$.
    Combining this with part~(i) identifies the minimum schedule length over
    $\K$ with $R_{\K}(\bz^{a})$. The value \eqref{eq_rank} of the complex rank
    of a monomial is the theorem of Carlini, Catalisano, and
    Geramita~\cite{CCG2012}; Appendix~\ref{sec_apolar} recalls the apolarity
    argument behind its lower bound.

    \subsubsection*{(iii) The roots-of-unity schedule attains the minimum}

    We use the conventions that an empty product equals $1$ and that a Cartesian
    product over an empty index set has one element, the empty tuple; these
    cover the case $n = 0$, in which \eqref{eq_rou} produces the single
    direction $e_{i_{0}}$ with coefficient $\nu!$. By part~(i) it suffices to
    prove the identity $\sum_{\zeta} c_{\zeta}(\bv_{\zeta} \cdot \bz)^{p} =
    p!\,\bz^{a}$. Fix $\beta$ with $\abs{\beta} = p$ and let $C_{\beta}$ denote
    the coefficient of $\bz^{\beta}$ on the left-hand side. Inserting
    \eqref{eq_rou} and applying the multinomial theorem gives
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_cbeta}
    \begin{aligned}
        C_{\beta} &= \frac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n} m_{j}} \binom{p}{\beta} \sum_{\zeta_{1} \in U_{m_{1}}} \cdots \sum_{\zeta_{n} \in U_{m_{n}}} \prod_{j=1}^{n} \zeta_{j}^{\beta_{j}+1} \\
        &= \frac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n} m_{j}} \binom{p}{\beta} \prod_{j=1}^{n} \Bigl(\sum_{\zeta_{j} \in U_{m_{j}}} \zeta_{j}^{\beta_{j}+1}\Bigr),
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    where the second equality separates the independent sums. By
    Lemma~\ref{lem_filter}, the $j$th factor of the product in \eqref{eq_cbeta}
    vanishes unless $m_{j} \mid \beta_{j}+1$, and $m_{j} = a_{j}+1$ turns this
    divisibility into the congruence $\beta_{j} \equiv a_{j} \pmod{m_{j}}$.
    Suppose $C_{\beta} \ne 0$, and write $\beta_{j} = a_{j} + t_{j}m_{j}$ with
    $t_{j} \in \N_{0}$ for $j = 1, \ldots, n$. The degree constraint
    $\abs{\beta} = p = \sum_{j=0}^{n} a_{j}$ then forces $\beta_{0} = a_{0} -
    \sum_{j=1}^{n} t_{j}m_{j}$. If some $t_{j}$ were positive, then $m_{j} =
    a_{j}+1 \ge a_{0}+1$ by the ordering of the exponents, whence $\beta_{0} \le
    a_{0} - m_{j} < 0$, contradicting $\beta_{0} \ge 0$. Every $t_{j}$ therefore
    vanishes, so $\beta_{j} = a_{j}$ for $j \ge 1$, and the degree constraint
    gives $\beta_{0} = a_{0}$ as well. Hence $C_{\beta} = 0$ for every $\beta
    \ne a$. For $\beta = a$ each inner sum in \eqref{eq_cbeta} equals $m_{j}$,
    the product cancels the prefactor, and
    \begin{equation*}
        C_{a} = \nu!\binom{p}{a} = \Bigl(\prod_{j=0}^{n} a_{j}!\Bigr) \frac{p!}{\prod_{j=0}^{n} a_{j}!} = p!\,.
    \end{equation*}
    The left-hand side of the identity therefore equals $p!\,\bz^{a}$, and the
    schedule has $\abs{U_{m_{1}} \times \cdots \times U_{m_{n}}} =
    \prod_{j=1}^{n} m_{j}$ directions.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm_main} converts a question about derivative evaluation into a
rank computation whose answer is the product \eqref{eq_rank}. The
minimizing directions and coefficients in \eqref{eq_rou} are roots of unity
scaled by $\nu!/R_{\C}(\bz^{a})$, so their magnitudes are known in advance.
Three consequences deserve separate mention.

\begin{remark}[The smallest instance]\label{rmk_special}
    For $\dal = \partial_{1}\partial_{2}$ we have $n = 1$, $a_{0} = a_{1} = 1$,
    $m_{1} = 2$, and $U_{2} = \{+1, -1\}$, so the roots of unity are real and
    \eqref{eq_rou} returns the two pairs $(\tfrac{1}{2}, e_{1}+e_{2})$ and
    $(-\tfrac{1}{2}, e_{1}-e_{2})$, that is, the elementary identity
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_two_point}
        \partial_{1}\partial_{2}u(\bx) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Tp(\bx; e_{1}+e_{2}) - \tfrac{1}{2}\Tp(\bx; e_{1}-e_{2}),
    \end{equation}
    in which the pure second derivatives cancel between the two probes.
    Theorem~\ref{thm_main} adds to \eqref{eq_two_point} the information that two
    evaluations are necessary as well as sufficient. Complex directions appear
    once two or more coordinates are active and some exponent is at least two.
    A pure power $\partial_{1}^{p}u$ uses one real direction, and a square-free
    monomial uses only the real pair $U_{2} = \{\pm 1\}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Entire activations are essential]\label{rmk_entire}
    The directions \eqref{eq_rou} are complex whenever some factor $U_{m_{j}}$
    with $j \ge 1$ has $m_{j} \ge 3$, so the function is then evaluated at
    complex arguments and its activations must admit a holomorphic extension
    there. The functions $\sinh$ and $\cosh$ are entire, and the recurrence
    \eqref{eq_jet_sinh} is an algebraic identity that holds verbatim on complex
    tensors. By contrast $\tanh$ is meromorphic, with poles at
    $\mathrm{i}\pi/2 + \mathrm{i}k\pi$ for $k \in \Z$, so its jet is defined only
    away from those points and the admissible directions depend on the activation
    input. The collocation measurements of Section~\ref{sec_experiments} use
    $\sinh$ at every order, including the order-two residuals whose directions
    remain real.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Scope of the rank reduction]\label{rmk_scope}
    Theorem~\ref{thm_main} concerns one monomial derivative at a time. For the
    square-free multi-index $a = (1, \ldots, 1)$ of order $p$ the rank
    \eqref{eq_rank} equals $2^{p-1}$, so the schedule is exponentially long; since
    the theorem gives a minimum, this is a statement about the problem and not about
    the construction, and no schedule of the form \eqref{eq_schedule} can be
    shorter. A differential operator that is a linear combination of monomial
    derivatives is evaluated by applying the schedule to each monomial. The
    stochastic Taylor derivative estimator~\cite{Shi2024stde} treats the strictly
    broader problem of an arbitrary contraction of the order-$p$ derivative
    tensor, with unbiased estimates whose variance is controlled by a sampling
    budget; the two approaches are complementary, and the schedule of
    Theorem~\ref{thm_main} is the deterministic minimum for one fixed
    multi-index.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Algorithm, gradients, and cost}\label{sec_algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} assembles the pieces. The schedule \eqref{eq_rou} is
built once, its directions are stacked into a single batch, and one jet pass
through the function returns the order-$p$ coefficient for every direction at
once; the reported value is then the coefficient-weighted sum. The schedule
tensors depend on $\nu$, the device, and the scalar type, but not on $\theta$.
The reference implementation builds them at call entry and includes that work in
the measured throughput of Section~\ref{sec_experiments}, while a caller that
reuses one multi-index may cache them. For a real-valued function the conjugate
pairing of the roots of unity makes the returned value real in exact arithmetic;
the implementation keeps the complex tensor and takes its real part only when
the target PDE is real-valued.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
  \caption{Single-monomial $\dal u(\bx)$ from a rank-optimal schedule and one batched Taylor jet.}
  \label{alg_waring}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \REQUIRE scalar network $u$ built from supported analytic primitives; point $\bx \in \R^{d}$; multi-index $\nu$ of order $p$
  \ENSURE the value $\dal u(\bx)$
  \STATE Relabel the active coordinates of $\nu$ as $i_{0}, \ldots, i_{n}$ with exponents $1 \le a_{0} \le \cdots \le a_{n}$.
  \STATE \label{alg_line_first} Set $m_{j} := a_{j}+1$ for $j = 1, \ldots, n$ and $\mathcal{I} := U_{m_{1}} \times \cdots \times U_{m_{n}}$.
  \STATE Initialize $\mathcal{S} \gets \emptyset$.
  \FOR{$\zeta = (\zeta_{1}, \ldots, \zeta_{n}) \in \mathcal{I}$}
      \STATE Set $\bv_{\zeta} \gets e_{i_{0}} + \sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}e_{i_{j}}$ and $c_{\zeta} \gets \nu!\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}\bigr)^{-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}$.
      \STATE Update $\mathcal{S} \gets \mathcal{S} \cup \{(c_{\zeta}, \bv_{\zeta})\}$.
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE \label{alg_line_last} Stack the directions of $\mathcal{S}$ into $V_{\mathcal{S}} \in \C^{\abs{\mathcal{S}} \times d}$.
  \STATE Form the batched input jet $\mathbf{J}_{0} \gets (\bx \text{ replicated}, V_{\mathcal{S}}, 0, \ldots, 0)$.
  \STATE Propagate $\mathbf{J}_{0}$ through $u$ by \eqref{eq_jet_affine}--\eqref{eq_jet_sinh} to obtain $\mathbf{J}_{L}$.
  \STATE Set $t \gets [\mathbf{J}_{L}]_{p}$, one order-$p$ coefficient per direction.
  \STATE \textbf{return} $\sum_{r=1}^{\abs{\mathcal{S}}} c_{r}t_{r}$.
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} must be differentiable in $\theta$, since its output
enters the residual term of \eqref{eq_pinn_loss}. Differentiating the activation
recurrences \eqref{eq_jet_tanh}--\eqref{eq_jet_sinh} by reverse-mode automatic
differentiation would rebuild inside the backward pass the nesting that the jet
was introduced to avoid. We therefore register each activation jet as a single
primitive with the closed-form adjoint
\begin{equation}\label{eq_vjp}
    g_{x}^{(m)} = \sum_{j=0}^{p-m} \overline{q_{j}}\,g_{y}^{(m+j)}, \quad q(\tau) := \phi'\bigl(x(\tau)\bigr),
\end{equation}
in which $q_{j}$ is the $j$th Taylor coefficient of $q$ and $g^{(m)}$ denotes
the cotangent of the order-$m$ coefficient. Equation~\eqref{eq_vjp} follows from
the chain rule applied to the jet of $\phi$ and uses the convention of a
real-valued loss for complex parameters, so that the conjugation is the
Hermitian adjoint of multiplication by $q_{j}$. The backward pass thus costs one
more triangular convolution per activation and never differentiates through the
recurrence itself.

The cost of Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} separates into a per-direction and a
per-schedule factor. For one direction, the jet stores $p+1$ coefficient tensors
per activation, an affine layer applies $W$ to each of them, and the activation
recurrences require $\sum_{k=1}^{p}O(k) = O(p^{2})$ elementwise products per
scalar. The total work and the directional storage then scale linearly in the
schedule length $R_{\C}(\bz^{a})$, which by Theorem~\ref{thm_main} is as small
as an exact directional formula can be. The rank reduction is an exact statement
about that factor and not about wall-clock time, which also depends on complex
arithmetic, kernel launch overhead, memory traffic, and whether parameter
gradients are required; for small widths or small batches these terms can
dominate. Section~\ref{sec_experiments} therefore reports measured throughput
alongside accuracy.

The schedule coefficients are known in closed form. The remaining error is
floating-point round-off from the Taylor recurrences, from complex arithmetic,
and from the final summation over directions. Section~\ref{sec_num_verify}
reports double-precision agreement through order six.

\section{Numerical experiments}\label{sec_experiments}

This section checks two properties of the schedule: agreement with nested
automatic differentiation in double precision, and the time and memory of one
residual evaluation, including the reverse-mode pass. The first comparison uses
no PDE. The second repeats the same evaluation inside an otherwise identical
collocation loop and records the time and memory of one step. Every quantity
below is a property of the derivative method on a fixed function, residual,
and optimizer.

\subsection{Verification of the derivative backend}\label{sec_num_verify}

All checks use double precision and are executable tests in the repository. The
roots-of-unity coefficient filter of Lemma~\ref{lem_filter} is evaluated against
every degree-four monomial in five variables for the active-exponent pattern
$(2,1,1)$, with a maximum admissible absolute coefficient error of $10^{-12}$.
The length of the schedule that the implementation builds is checked against the
closed-form rank \eqref{eq_rank} for every pattern of Table~\ref{tab_patterns}.

The Waring jet of Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} is then compared with nested
automatic differentiation on real-parameter networks using the $\tanh$,
logistic, $\sin$, and $\sinh$ activations, for the patterns $(2,2)$,
$(1,1,1)$, and $(3)$. Values must agree to relative tolerance
$2 \times 10^{-10}$ and absolute tolerance $2 \times 10^{-11}$, and the
imaginary remainder of a real target must not exceed $2 \times 10^{-11}$.
Parameter gradients of a squared derivative loss are compared at tolerances
$2 \times 10^{-9}$ relative and $2 \times 10^{-11}$ absolute, on a real
$\tanh$ network and on a complex $\sinh$ network; additional analytic checks
cover the orders $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, and $6$ for a scalar complex $\sinh$
network. Every check passes at the tolerance stated for it. These tests
establish that the implementation computes what Theorem~\ref{thm_main}
predicts.

\subsection{A controlled comparison of derivative backends}\label{sec_num_protocol}

The remaining study isolates the derivative backend. Protocol \texttt{jsc\_v3}
runs two lines that share the network, the loss, the optimizer, the collocation
points, the evaluation rule, the seeds, and the wall-clock budget, and differ in
one respect: how each monomial derivative of the residual is evaluated. The
first line uses Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring}, that is, the rank-optimal complex
schedule evaluated by one batched Taylor jet, and is labeled \emph{jet} below.
The second evaluates the same derivative of the same network by nested
reverse-mode automatic differentiation, with the graph retained after every
coordinate derivative, and is labeled \emph{nested}. Both lines are the same
complex-parameter $\sinh$ network with four hidden layers of width $H = 128$,
hence the same 100{,}098 trainable real degrees of freedom, the same first-layer
scale, and the same \texttt{complex128} arithmetic. Both use Adam with initial
learning rate $10^{-3}$ and cosine decay, 4096 fixed interior points, 512 fixed
boundary points, paired per-seed collocation, the same per-setting boundary
weights, and a common held-out set of 8192 points. Each line is run for three
seeds with a budget of 1000 seconds per run.

A \emph{setting} is one problem together with one value of its sweep parameter,
and the protocol covers nine: the two-dimensional polyharmonic problem at orders
$2m \in \{2,4,6\}$, the radial chirp at $a \in \{1,2,3\}$, and the time-harmonic
Maxwell problem at $a \in \{2,4,6\}$, all defined as in
Section~\ref{sec_num_problems}. All 54 runs were produced by one source commit
on one NVIDIA H20 GPU with PyTorch 2.5.1 and CUDA 12.1, so unlike a comparison
assembled from separate campaigns, timings here are comparable across settings
as well as within one. Cost is the measured time per optimizer step together
with the peak memory allocated by a run, and accuracy is the held-out relative
$L^{2}$ error
\begin{equation}\label{eq_rel_error}
    \mathcal{E}_{L^{2}} := \frac{\norm{u_{\theta}-u_{\star}}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\norm{u_{\star}}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}},
\end{equation}
evaluated on that held-out set against the exact solution $u_{\star}$. A setting
enters the tables only after automated checks confirm all six method-seed rows,
finite metrics, nonempty monotone histories, and the protocol metadata.

\subsection{Benchmark problems}\label{sec_num_problems}

The three families place different demands on the derivative. For the
polyharmonic problem on $(-1,1)^{2}$ we solve
\begin{equation}\label{eq_num_poly}
    \Delta^{m}u = (-2\pi^{2})^{m}u_{\star} \quad\text{in }(-1,1)^{2}, \quad u_{\star}(\bx) = \sin(\pi x_{1})\sin(\pi x_{2}),
\end{equation}
at the orders $2m \in \{2,4,6\}$, with the Navier boundary targets $u =
u_{\star}$ and $\Delta^{j}u = (-2\pi^{2})^{j}u_{\star}$ for $j = 1, \ldots,
m-1$. Those traces vanish on $\partial(-1,1)^{2}$ because $u_{\star}$ does.
This family is the reason the derivative order appears as a variable: expanding
$\Delta^{m}$ in two dimensions produces the patterns $(2m)$ and, for $m \ge 2$,
the mixed patterns whose schedules Table~\ref{tab_patterns} lists. At $2m = 4$
the mixed term is $\partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}$, which \eqref{eq_rou}
evaluates with three directions; at $2m = 6$ the mixed terms are
$\partial_{1}^{4}\partial_{2}^{2}$ and $\partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{4}$,
which take five each.

The other two families sit at order two and sweep a frequency instead. On
$(-1,1)^{2}$ the radial chirp is
\begin{equation}\label{eq_num_chirp}
    -\Delta u + u = f, \quad u_{\star}(x,y) = \sin\Bigl(\frac{a\pi}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})\Bigr),
\end{equation}
with Dirichlet data from $u_{\star}$, $a \in \{1,2,3\}$, and the manufactured
source $f = (a\pi)^{2}r^{2}\sin\phi - 2a\pi\cos\phi + \sin\phi$, where $\phi :=
a\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})/2$ and $r^{2} := x^{2}+y^{2}$. Its local radial wavenumber
$\abs{\nabla\phi} = a\pi r$ varies over the domain, so the target is not a
separable Fourier mode. For a two-dimensional transverse-magnetic mode,
Maxwell's equations reduce to a scalar complex Helmholtz equation for the
out-of-plane field $E$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq_num_maxwell}
    \Delta E + \kappa^{2}E = f, \quad \kappa^{2} = (a\pi)^{2}(1+\mathrm{i}\beta), \quad E_{\star} = \exp\bigl(\mathrm{i}a\pi(x+y)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\beta = 0.2$, exact Dirichlet data, $a \in \{2,4,6\}$, and $f =
[-2(a\pi)^{2}+\kappa^{2}]E_{\star}$. The Maxwell family has a complex-valued
solution. The measurements already propagate complex tensors through a
complex-parameter $\sinh$ network, so a complex residual uses the same jet as
the real ones. Since both families are of order two, every monomial term of
their residuals is a pure power, which the schedule evaluates with a single
real direction. They therefore isolate the cost of the Taylor jet on one
direction.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec_concl}

The shortest exact formula for a high-order derivative, in directional Taylor
coefficients of the same order, is the Waring decomposition of the corresponding
monomial. Carlini, Catalisano, and Geramita evaluate the complex length as
\eqref{eq_rank}, and a roots-of-unity formula attains it. No shorter linear
combination of order-$p$ directional Taylor coefficients exists; the two-point
identity for $\partial_{1}\partial_{2}$ is the length-two case.

Combined with a batched Taylor jet and a closed-form vector-Jacobian product,
the schedule evaluates the derivative exactly and remains differentiable in
$\theta$. Double-precision tests reproduce nested automatic differentiation for
values and for parameter gradients through order six. Measured against nested
differentiation, with the function, the loss, and the optimizer held fixed, the
schedule reduces the time of one evaluation by factors of about $1.4$, $2.3$,
and $4.1$
at derivative orders two, four, and six, and its peak memory by about $1.5$,
$3.1$, and $6.5$; on the higher-order problems the evaluations thus gained lower
the error reached within a fixed budget. The two mechanisms behind that growth
are separable in the measurements: Taylor-mode evaluation removes the nested
graph at every order, and the rank reduction shortens the schedule wherever an
index repeats.

The schedule is shortest for repeated-index multi-indices and exponentially long
for square-free ones: Theorem~\ref{thm_main} shows that this length is the
minimum. At low derivative order a cheaper derivative also buys little in a
complete run, since the derivative is not what limits it. Natural continuations
are a rank-aware expansion of an operator that reuses directions across its
terms, a conditioning analysis of the schedule at high order and in reduced
precision, and the extension of the same apolarity argument to derivative
tensors contracted against a fixed structured family.

\section{Schedules, filters, and the apolar bound}\label{sec_schedules}

This appendix collects the roots-of-unity filter used in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm_main}, the explicit schedules for the derivative patterns that
occur in Section~\ref{sec_experiments}, and the apolarity argument behind the
rank formula \eqref{eq_rank}. The formulas of Appendix~\ref{sec_lowschedules}
are those the implementation evaluates.

\subsection{Roots-of-unity filter}\label{sec_filter}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem_filter}
    For every integer $m \ge 1$ and every integer $k$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_filter}
        \sum_{\zeta \in U_{m}} \zeta^{k} = m\,\mathbf{1}_{m \mid k}.
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\omega := \exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}/m)$, so that every element of $U_{m}$ is
    $\omega^{\ell}$ for exactly one $\ell \in \{0, \ldots, m-1\}$ and
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{\zeta \in U_{m}} \zeta^{k} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\bigl(\omega^{k}\bigr)^{\ell}.
    \end{equation*}
    If $m \mid k$, then $\omega^{k} = 1$ and the sum equals $m$. If $m \nmid k$,
    then $\omega^{k} \ne 1$, and the finite geometric series gives
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\bigl(\omega^{k}\bigr)^{\ell} = \frac{1-\omega^{km}}{1-\omega^{k}} = 0,
    \end{equation*}
    since $\omega^{m} = 1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Explicit low-order schedules}\label{sec_lowschedules}

The mixed terms of the polyharmonic residual \eqref{eq_num_poly} and the pure
powers of the chirp and Maxwell residuals use the following instances of
\eqref{eq_rou}.

For a pure power $\dal = \partial_{1}^{p}$ we have $n = 0$, the schedule has one
direction, and $\Tp$ is evaluated once,
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_pure}
    \partial_{1}^{p}u(\bx) = p!\,\Tp(\bx; e_{1}).
\end{equation}

For the fourth-order mixed term $\dal = \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}$ we
have $n = 1$, $a = (2,2)$, and $m_{1} = 3$; with $\omega :=
\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}/3)$ and $U_{3} = \{1, \omega, \omega^{2}\}$, formula
\eqref{eq_rou} gives the three directions
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_22}
    \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}u(\bx) = \frac{4}{3}\sum_{\zeta \in U_{3}} \zeta\,\Tp(\bx;\, e_{1}+\zeta e_{2}).
\end{equation}

For the sixth-order mixed term $\dal = \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{4}$ we
have $n = 1$, $a = (2,4)$, and $m_{1} = 5$; with $U_{5}$ the fifth roots of
unity, \eqref{eq_rou} takes the coordinate of smaller exponent as base and
gives the five directions
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_24}
    \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{4}u(\bx) = \frac{48}{5}\sum_{\zeta \in U_{5}} \zeta\,\Tp(\bx;\, e_{1}+\zeta e_{2}).
\end{equation}
The companion term $\partial_{1}^{4}\partial_{2}^{2}$ is the same formula with
the two coordinates exchanged. The equivalent polynomial identity
\eqref{eq_bridge} for \eqref{eq_sched_24} reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_24_poly}
    6!\,z_{0}^{2}z_{1}^{4} = \frac{48}{5}\sum_{\zeta \in U_{5}} \zeta\,(z_{0}+\zeta z_{1})^{6},
\end{equation}
and it can be checked directly: the coefficient of
$z_{0}^{\beta_{0}}z_{1}^{\beta_{1}}$ on the right-hand side contains the factor
$\sum_{\zeta \in U_{5}}\zeta^{1+\beta_{1}}$, which by Lemma~\ref{lem_filter} is
nonzero only when $\beta_{1} \equiv 4 \pmod{5}$.
Together with $\beta_{0}+\beta_{1} = 6$ this forces $\beta = (2,4)$, and the
surviving constant is $\tfrac{48}{5} \cdot \tfrac{6!}{2!\,4!} \cdot 5 = 720 =
6!$. The three-variable pattern $(2,2,2)$ of Table~\ref{tab_patterns} is the
same construction with two cube-root factors and nine directions.

\subsection{Apolar form of the rank bound}\label{sec_apolar}

The lower bound in \eqref{eq_rank} comes from apolarity, which we recall for
completeness. For a homogeneous $F \in \C[\bz]_{p}$, let $F^{\perp}$ denote its
apolar ideal, that is, the ideal of differential operators annihilating $F$, and
call a finite subscheme $X \subset \mathbb{P}^{n}$ apolar to $F$ when $I_{X}
\subset F^{\perp}$. The Waring rank then admits the characterization
\begin{equation}\label{eq_apolar}
    R_{\C}(F) = \min\bigl\{\deg X : X \subset \mathbb{P}^{n} \text{ finite, reduced, and apolar to } F\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
For the monomial $F = \bz^{a}$ the apolar ideal is the complete intersection
\begin{equation}\label{eq_apolar_ideal}
    (\bz^{a})^{\perp} = \bigl(\partial_{0}^{a_{0}+1}, \ldots, \partial_{n}^{a_{n}+1}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the Hilbert function of the quotient $\C[\partial]/(\bz^{a})^{\perp}$ bounds
the degree of an apolar subscheme from below by the product
$\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}$ of \eqref{eq_rank}; see~\cite{IK1999,Landsberg2012} for
the general theory and~\cite{CCG2012} for the monomial case. The construction
\eqref{eq_rou} exhibits a reduced apolar subscheme of exactly that degree, so
the bound is attained.
