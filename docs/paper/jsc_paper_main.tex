\title{Exact Single-Monomial High-Order Partial Derivatives via\\
       Waring Theory and Taylor Directional Derivatives}
\author{}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We present a deterministic Taylor-jet backend for evaluating one fixed
high-order mixed partial derivative of a neural-network surrogate, a
workload that appears repeatedly in scientific machine learning and
high-order physics-informed neural networks. The central observation is
that a single multi-index derivative is a coefficient of a directional
Taylor polynomial; hence the problem of designing a shortest exact set
of directional probes is equivalent to a Waring decomposition of the
corresponding monomial. The Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita theorem gives
the minimum number of complex directions in closed form and an explicit
roots-of-unity construction. We combine this schedule with Taylor-mode
propagation through multilayer perceptrons, using the entire activation
\(\sinh\) when complex parameters are used, to obtain an exact
floating-point implementation that is differentiable with respect to
network parameters. Numerical experiments on direct derivative tasks
and a four-dimensional manufactured sixth-order PDE compare the method
with nested automatic differentiation and real polarization. The
results confirm round-off-level agreement with the direct reference and
show that, for repeated-index multi-indices, the Waring schedule reduces
the number of Taylor directions while remaining competitive in measured
wall-clock time. Because \(\sinh\) is entire on \(\C\), the same backend
also makes \emph{complex-parameter} \(\sinh\) networks high-order
differentiable without any runtime dtype conversion; we study these as a
PINN ansatz for oscillatory and high-order problems and benchmark them,
under matched parameter counts and a shared training protocol, against
strong real high-frequency baselines (Fourier features, SIREN, MscaleDNN)
on a literature-grounded suite of nine PDE families. The complex network
is competitive-to-superior on the majority of instances, with the largest
gains on balanced repeated-index high-order operators and on genuinely
complex-valued problems.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Physics-informed neural networks; high-order
automatic differentiation; Waring decomposition; apolarity; Taylor-mode
AD; single-monomial partial derivative.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{MSC 2020.} 65D25, 68T07, 14A25, 35J30.
\end{abstract}

% =========================================================
\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================

\subsection{The problem}

Many scientific machine-learning workflows ask, repeatedly during
training, for the value of a single fixed high-order mixed partial
derivative of a neural-network surrogate. Examples include
physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) \cite{Raissi2019} for
high-order PDEs (Korteweg--de Vries, Cahn--Hilliard, biharmonic,
polyharmonic), neural-network variational Monte Carlo
\cite{Pfau2020,Hermann2020}, and stochastic optimal control via the
Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman equation.

The naive evaluation chains successive reverse-mode differentiations
through the network. The resulting autograd graph deepens linearly
with both the differentiation order and the network depth, and the
wall-clock cost becomes the bottleneck of training as soon as the
order moves beyond two or three. Three classes of acceleration
techniques have been proposed:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Forward-mode / Taylor-mode automatic differentiation}
\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008}, which propagates truncated Taylor
coefficients through every primitive of the network in a single forward
pass and avoids the autograd-graph chaining overhead.
\item \textbf{Stochastic estimators} \cite{Hu2023sdgd,Shi2024stde},
which write the target operator as an expectation over random tangent
vectors and estimate it by Monte Carlo sampling.
\item \textbf{Operator-specific specializations}
\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian,Gao2023folx} for the Laplacian (the trace
of the Hessian), exploiting the fact that the trace collapses cleanly
through linear layers.
\end{enumerate}
Each has limitations. Forward-mode AD by itself does not say how to
combine information from several directions. Stochastic estimators
introduce a variance that depends on the sampling budget. And
operator-specific methods do not extend to non-trace multi-indices.

This paper develops a fourth, complementary route: an exact
deterministic backend for one fixed multi-index at a time, based on
identifying the directional schedule problem with a classical
combinatorial-algebraic problem -- the Waring decomposition of a
monomial.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Bridge theorem (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).} We prove that
a directional schedule -- a finite linear combination of directional
Taylor coefficients reproducing the target derivative for every smooth
input -- exists with a given direction count if and only if a single
monomial of degree equal to the differentiation order admits a
Waring decomposition of that length. Consequently, the smallest
possible direction count equals the Waring rank of the corresponding
monomial.

\item \textbf{Closed-form schedule (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).} The
Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita rank theorem \cite{CCG2012} and its
roots-of-unity construction yield, for any fixed multi-index, an
explicit complex schedule whose direction count attains the
information-theoretic minimum over the complex field. For some
repeated-index patterns this strictly improves on classical real
polarization; for square-free patterns the direction counts coincide.

\item \textbf{End-to-end algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).} We pair
the schedule above with a forward-mode Taylor-jet propagation through
\(\sinh\)-activated multilayer perceptrons. Because \(\sinh\) is entire
on the complex plane, the jet rules are well-defined on complex inputs
and complex parameters, and the resulting algorithm is exact up to
floating-point round-off.

\item \textbf{Scientific-computing evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).} We benchmark direct derivative
queries and a four-dimensional manufactured sixth-order PDE. The
comparison uses the two implemented deterministic baselines -- direct
nested automatic differentiation and real polarization with Taylor jets
-- and reports both direction counts and measured wall-clock costs.
Within a fixed wall-clock budget, the complex Waring backend gives the
best result in the single tested PDE run; we report this as fixed-budget
evidence rather than a statistical superiority claim.

\item \textbf{Oscillatory expressivity study
(Section~\ref{sec:osc}).} The entirety of
\(\sinh\) on \(\C\) lets the same backend differentiate
\emph{complex-parameter} \(\sinh\) networks to arbitrary order with no
dtype casting in the inner loop. We treat such a network as a PINN ansatz
and ask whether the complex value field is itself more expressive on
oscillatory and high-order targets. On a literature-grounded suite of nine
PDE families -- evaluated under matched real-parameter counts, matched
frequency initialization, and one shared training protocol -- the complex
\(\sinh\) network is competitive-to-superior against Fourier-feature,
SIREN, and MscaleDNN baselines on most instances, with the clearest
advantage on balanced repeated-index high-order operators
(e.g.\ \(\Delta^{3}\), mixed plate modes) and on genuinely complex-valued
problems (nonlinear Schr\"odinger, lossy Maxwell). We report the advantage
and its limits honestly, including the families where the gap closes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

The \emph{rank advantage} of this work is specific to one fixed
multi-index: it shortens the directional schedule of a single
repeated-index mixed partial. Operator sums such as the Laplacian or the
polyharmonic operator \(\Delta^{m}\) are still evaluated exactly by the
same jet machinery -- each summand is one (rank-one) directional jet and
the residual is their sum -- but for such sums the Waring rank offers no
shortening, and dedicated trace-collapsing techniques
\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian,Gao2023folx} can be more efficient when only
a Laplacian is needed. The oscillatory study of
Section~\ref{sec:osc} uses the backend in
this summed, exact-but-not-shortened mode wherever the operator is a sum,
and exploits the genuine rank advantage on the mixed high-order terms. We
also do not pursue stochastic estimation; its accuracy-cost tradeoffs are
complementary to ours \cite{Shi2024stde}.

\subsection{Relation to existing approaches}

The method sits between general automatic differentiation and
operator-specific scientific-computing accelerators. Taylor-mode AD
\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008,TaylorDiff2023} supplies the
univariate directional derivatives, but it does not determine which
finite set of directions extracts a prescribed mixed partial. Real
polarization provides a deterministic schedule with real directions, at
the cost of up to \(2^{p-1}\) coalesced probes. Stochastic derivative
estimators \cite{Hu2023sdgd,Shi2024stde} address broader tensor
contractions through sampling and are therefore complementary rather
than direct replacements for an exact single-monomial schedule.
Trace-specialized methods for Laplacians
\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian,Gao2023folx} exploit operator sums and are
outside the single-monomial setting. Our contribution is to use the
monomial Waring rank to make the deterministic direction schedule
minimal over \(\C\) for the fixed multi-index problem.

\subsection{Notation}

Throughout the paper, \(u:\R^{d}\to\R\) denotes a sufficiently smooth
scalar field. A multi-index of order \(p\) is an ordered tuple
\(\alpha=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{p}\), encoding the
operator
\begin{equation}
\dal \;:=\; \partial_{i_{1}}\,\partial_{i_{2}}\,\cdots\,\partial_{i_{p}}.
\end{equation}
The \emph{active coordinates} of \(\alpha\) are its distinct entries,
relabelled as \(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}\) so that their multiplicities
\(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\) satisfy
\(1\le a_{0}\le\cdots\le a_{n}\) (ties may be broken by the original
coordinate index). This ordering is not, in general, the coordinate
ordering; it singles out a minimum-multiplicity variable \(i_{0}\), as
required by the monomial Waring rank formula below. We set
\begin{equation}
p \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n} a_{j},
\qquad
\alpha! \;=\; \prod_{j=0}^{n} a_{j}!,
\qquad
m_{j} \;=\; a_{j}+1.
\end{equation}

The \emph{active subspace} \(V\) is the linear span of the active
coordinate directions,
\begin{equation}
V \;:=\; \mathrm{span}\{e_{i_{0}},\ldots,e_{i_{n}}\} \;\subset\; \R^{d};
\end{equation}
since \(\dal\) is insensitive to coordinates outside the active set,
all directional probes \(v\) below may be taken in \(V\).

The directional Taylor coefficient of order \(p\) at \(x\) along
\(v\in V\) is
\begin{equation}
\Tp(x;v) \;:=\; \frac{1}{p!}\,
   \frac{d^{p}}{d\tau^{p}}\,u(x+\tau v)\Big|_{\tau=0}
       \;=\; \frac{1}{p!}\,D^{p}u(x)[v,\ldots,v].
\label{eq:Tp-def}
\end{equation}

Throughout the paper, the default scalar field is the real field \(\R\);
we write \(\R[z]_{p}\) for the space of homogeneous polynomials of
degree \(p\) in \(n+1\) variables \(z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\) over
\(\R\). The complex extension \(\C[z]_{p}\) is needed only in
Section~\ref{ssec:waring-rank}, where the closed-form schedule of
Theorem~\ref{thm:rou} uses complex coefficients and complex linear
forms; we introduce it explicitly there.

% =========================================================
\section{Single-Monomial Partials as Polynomial Coefficient Extraction}
\label{sec:coeff}
% =========================================================

\subsection{The generating polynomial}

For \(z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\in\R^{n+1}\) parametrizing the active
subspace via \(v=\sum_{j}z_{j}e_{i_{j}}\), the \emph{generating polynomial}
of \(u\) at \(x\) is
\begin{equation}
Q_{u}(z) \;:=\; \Tp\!\Big(x;\;\sum_{j=0}^{n}z_{j}e_{i_{j}}\Big),
\qquad z\in\R^{n+1}.
\label{eq:Qu-def}
\end{equation}
By construction \(Q_{u}\) is a degree-\(p\) homogeneous polynomial in
\(z\); the next lemma identifies its coefficients with the partial
derivatives of \(u\).

\begin{lemma}[Generating polynomial expansion]\label{lem:Qu-expand}
For \(u\in C^{p}\) on a neighborhood of \(x\),
\begin{equation}
Q_{u}(z) \;=\; \sum_{|\beta|=p}\frac{\partial^{\beta}u(x)}{\beta!}\,z^{\beta},
\label{eq:Qu-expand}
\end{equation}
where \(\beta\) ranges over multi-indices on the active variables and
\(\partial^{\beta}=\partial_{i_{0}}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{n}}^{\beta_{n}}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By the symmetric multilinearity of \(D^{p}u(x)\) and the multinomial
theorem,
\[
  D^{p}u(x)\Big[\textstyle\sum_{j}z_{j}e_{i_{j}},\ldots,
                \sum_{j}z_{j}e_{i_{j}}\Big]
  \;=\; \sum_{|\beta|=p}\frac{p!}{\beta!}\,z^{\beta}\,\partial^{\beta}u(x).
\]
Dividing by \(p!\) yields \eqref{eq:Qu-expand}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:coef-extract}
\(\dal u(x)=\alpha!\,[z^{\alpha}]\,Q_{u}(z)\), where \([z^{\alpha}]\)
extracts the coefficient of the monomial \(z^{\alpha}\).
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Directional schedules}

A \emph{directional schedule} of length \(R\) for \(\dal\) is a finite
collection \((c_{r},v_{r})_{r=1}^{R}\subset\R\times V\) such that
\begin{equation}
\dal u(x) \;=\; \sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\,\Tp(x;v_{r}),
\qquad \forall\, u\in C^{p}_{x}.
\label{eq:dir-schedule}
\end{equation}

Because the schedule is required to hold for every \(u\in C^{p}_{x}\),
the \(p\)-jet of \(u\) at \(x\) may be prescribed arbitrarily on the
active variables: for any coefficients \(q_{\beta}\), the polynomial
\(\sum_{\beta}q_{\beta}\prod_{j}(y_{i_{j}}-x_{i_{j}})^{\beta_{j}}\)
has \(\partial^{\beta}u(x)=\beta!\,q_{\beta}\). This observation lets us
test identities coefficient by coefficient in \(\R[z]_{p}\).

The central design question of this paper is then:
\emph{What is the minimum length \(R^{\ast}\) of a directional schedule
\eqref{eq:dir-schedule}, and how is one constructed?}
Section~\ref{sec:bridge} reduces this to a classical algebraic question.

% =========================================================
\section{Equivalence to Monomial Waring Decomposition}
\label{sec:bridge}
% =========================================================

\subsection{The bridge theorem}

For \(v\in V\), write \(v=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}e_{i_{j}}\) in active
coordinates. We pair this direction with the polynomial variable
\(z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\) by
\(v\!\cdot\!z=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}z_{j}\).

\begin{theorem}[Bridge]\label{thm:bridge}
Let \(R\in\N\), \(c_{r}\in\R\), \(v_{r}\in V\), \(r=1,\ldots,R\). The
following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=*]
\item (Directional schedule) \;
\(\dal u(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\Tp(x;v_{r})\)\quad
\(\forall\,u\in C^{p}_{x}\).
\item (Polynomial identity in \(\R[z]_{p}\)) \;
\(p!\,z^{\alpha}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}(v_{r}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write \(v_{r}=(v_{r,0},\ldots,v_{r,n})\) in active coordinates. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:Qu-expand},
\[
  \Tp(x;v_{r})
  =\sum_{|\beta|=p}\frac{\partial^{\beta}u(x)}{\beta!}\,v_{r}^{\beta}.
\]
Substituting this expansion into statement (1), and using that the
order-\(p\) active jet of \(u\) at \(x\) can be prescribed arbitrarily,
shows that (1) is equivalent to the finite-dimensional coefficient system
\begingroup\renewcommand{\theHequation}{eqstar}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\,v_{r}^{\beta}
\;=\; \alpha!\,\delta_{\alpha\beta},
\qquad \forall\,\beta\in\Z_{\ge 0}^{n+1}\text{ with }|\beta|=p.
\tag{$\star$}\label{eq:star}
\end{equation}
\endgroup
On the other hand, expanding statement (2) gives
\((v_{r}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}=\sum_{|\beta|=p}\binom{p}{\beta}v_{r}^{\beta}z^{\beta}\)
and hence, after comparing coefficients of \(z^{\beta}\),
\[
\binom{p}{\beta}\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}\,v_{r}^{\beta}
\;=\; p!\,\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad\forall\,|\beta|=p.
\]
Using \(p!/\binom{p}{\beta}=\beta!\) and
\(\beta!\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}=\alpha!\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\), this is
exactly \eqref{eq:star}. Thus (1) and (2) are equivalent.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Waring rank]\label{def:waring-rank}
For a homogeneous polynomial \(F\in\R[z]_{p}\), its \emph{real Waring
rank} is
\[
R_{\R}(F)\;:=\;\min\Big\{R:\;F=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{r}L_{r}(z)^{p},\;
c_{r}\in\R,\;L_{r}\in\R[z]_{1}\Big\}.
\]
The \emph{complex Waring rank} \(R_{\C}(F)\) is defined identically
with \(\R\) replaced by \(\C\); since \(\R\subset\C\),
\(R_{\C}(F)\le R_{\R}(F)\).
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:R-equals-rank}
The minimum length \(R^{\ast}\) of a directional schedule for \(\dal\)
equals the real Waring rank \(R_{\R}(z^{\alpha})\) of the monomial.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Complex extension]\label{rem:complex-extension}
Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} and its proof go through verbatim if the
coefficients \(c_{r}\) and the directions \(v_{r}\) are allowed to take
values in \(\C\) and \(\C^{n+1}\), and the polynomial identity is read
in \(\C[z]_{p}\). The minimum length of such a complex schedule then
equals \(R_{\C}(z^{\alpha})\). The next subsection gives this complex
rank in closed form.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Waring rank of a monomial}\label{ssec:waring-rank}

The closed-form rank over \(\C\) is classical. (Problem (P)/(Q) is posed
over \(\R\) by default; passing to \(\C\) can only decrease the rank, so
\(R_{\C}\le R_{\R}\).)

\begin{theorem}[Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita \cite{CCG2012}]
\label{thm:CCG}
For \(z^{\alpha}=z_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots z_{n}^{a_{n}}\) with
\(1\le a_{0}\le\cdots\le a_{n}\),
\begin{equation}
R_{\C}(z^{\alpha}) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}+1)
                     \;=\; \frac{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(a_{j}+1)}{a_{0}+1}.
\label{eq:CCG-rank}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The lower bound, sketched in \cite{CCG2012}, uses the apolar ideal
\(I_{z^{\alpha}}=(\partial_{0}^{a_{0}+1},\ldots,\partial_{n}^{a_{n}+1})\)
(a complete intersection); we recall the argument in
Appendix~\ref{app:apolar-pairing}. The upper bound is constructive.

\begin{theorem}[Roots-of-unity directional schedule]\label{thm:rou}
Let \(m_{j}=a_{j}+1\) for \(j=1,\ldots,n\), and for each tuple
\(\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n})\) with \(\zeta_{j}^{m_{j}}=1\) define
\begin{equation}
v_{\zeta}=e_{i_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}e_{i_{j}},
\qquad
c_{\zeta}=\frac{\alpha!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}.
\label{eq:rou-coeff}
\end{equation}
Then \(\dal u(x)=\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}\,\Tp(x;v_{\zeta})\) for every
\(u\in C^{p}_{x}\). The schedule has length
\(\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}=R_{\C}(z^{\alpha})\) and is therefore length-optimal
over \(\C\).
\end{theorem}

The proof, given in Appendix~\ref{app:rou-proof}, verifies condition (2)
of Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} directly via the roots-of-unity filter
\(\sum_{\zeta^{m}=1}\zeta^{k}=m\,\mathbf{1}_{m\mid k}\).

To illustrate how Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} couples the analytic side
\eqref{eq:dir-schedule} (problem~(P)) with the algebraic side
\(p!\,z^{\alpha}=\sum_{r}c_{r}(v_{r}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\) (problem~(Q)), we
work out two examples: a low-order one for which both sides reduce to
hand-checkable identities, and a higher-order repeated-index one that
exhibits the full strength of the complex Waring construction.

\begin{example}[\(p=2\), \(\alpha=(1,1)\), real schedule of length \(2\)]
\label{ex:u12}
For \(\alpha=(1,1)\) we have \(n=1\), \(a_{0}=a_{1}=1\), \(m_{1}=2\),
\(U_{2}=\{+1,-1\}\). Equation~\eqref{eq:rou-coeff} produces the schedule
\begin{equation}
\big(c_{+1},v_{+1}\big)=\big(\tfrac{1}{2},\,e_{1}+e_{2}\big),
\qquad
\big(c_{-1},v_{-1}\big)=\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\,e_{1}-e_{2}\big).
\label{eq:ex-u12-schedule}
\end{equation}

\emph{Verification on the analytic side.} Expanding the directional
Taylor coefficient
\(\Tp(x;v)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(u_{11}v_{1}^{2}+2u_{12}v_{1}v_{2}+u_{22}v_{2}^{2}\bigr)\),
\[
\tfrac{1}{2}\Tp(x;e_{1}+e_{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}\Tp(x;e_{1}-e_{2})
=\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl[(u_{11}+2u_{12}+u_{22})-(u_{11}-2u_{12}+u_{22})\bigr]
=u_{12}(x).
\]

\emph{Verification on the algebraic side.} The equivalent polynomial
identity in \(\R[z]_{2}\) reads
\[
2!\,z_{0}z_{1}
\;=\;
\tfrac{1}{2}(z_{0}+z_{1})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(z_{0}-z_{1})^{2}
\;=\;
\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(z_{0}^{2}+2z_{0}z_{1}+z_{1}^{2})-(z_{0}^{2}-2z_{0}z_{1}+z_{1}^{2})\bigr]
\;=\;2\,z_{0}z_{1},
\]
matching \(p!\,z^{\alpha}=2\,z_{0}z_{1}\). The two checks coincide via
the bridge of Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[\(p=6\), \(\alpha=(2,2,2)\), complex schedule of length \(9\)]
\label{ex:u112233}
Consider \(u_{112233}=\partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}\partial_{3}^{2}u\),
i.e.\ \(\alpha=(2,2,2)\). Here \(n=2\), \(a_{0}=a_{1}=a_{2}=2\),
\(m_{1}=m_{2}=3\); take \(\omega=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\) and
\(U_{3}=\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}\). Equation~\eqref{eq:rou-coeff}
produces \(R_{\C}(z^{(2,2,2)})=9\) directions: for each
\((\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in U_{3}\times U_{3}\),
\begin{equation}
v_{\zeta}\;=\;e_{1}+\zeta_{1}e_{2}+\zeta_{2}e_{3},
\qquad
c_{\zeta}\;=\;\frac{(2!)^{3}}{3\cdot 3}\,\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}
\;=\;\frac{8}{9}\,\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}.
\label{eq:ex-u112233-schedule}
\end{equation}

\emph{Analytic side.} The directional schedule reads
\begin{equation}
u_{112233}(x)
\;=\;
\frac{8}{9}\!\!\sum_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in U_{3}}\!\!
   \zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\,
   \Tp\!\bigl(x;\,e_{1}+\zeta_{1}e_{2}+\zeta_{2}e_{3}\bigr).
\label{eq:ex-u112233-P}
\end{equation}

\emph{Algebraic side.} The equivalent polynomial identity in \(\C[z]_{6}\) is
\begin{equation}
6!\,z_{0}^{2}z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}
\;=\;
\frac{8}{9}\!\!\sum_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in U_{3}}\!\!
   \zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\,(z_{0}+\zeta_{1}z_{1}+\zeta_{2}z_{2})^{6}.
\label{eq:ex-u112233-Q}
\end{equation}
That \eqref{eq:ex-u112233-Q} holds is verified by the roots-of-unity
filter (Lemma~\ref{lem:rou-filter}): expanding the right-hand side
multinomially and collecting the coefficient of \(z_{0}^{\beta_{0}}
z_{1}^{\beta_{1}}z_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\) reduces to the product
\(\prod_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{\zeta_{j}\in U_{3}}\zeta_{j}^{1+\beta_{j}}\),
which is non-zero exactly when \(\beta_{j}\equiv 2\pmod{3}\) for
\(j=1,2\); together with \(\sum_{j}\beta_{j}=6\) this forces
\(\beta=(2,2,2)\), and the leading constant
\(\tfrac{8}{9}\cdot\tfrac{6!}{2!\,2!\,2!}\cdot 9=720=6!\) gives the
right normalisation.

By comparison, the coalesced antipodally merged real polarization
schedule used in our baseline requires 13 distinct nonzero directions
for the same \(u_{112233}(x)\); the complex Waring schedule above

\section{Taylor-Mode Evaluation and Backend Dispatch}
\label{sec:impl}
% =========================================================

\subsection{The Taylor jet for \texorpdfstring{Linear--\(\sinh\)}{Linear-sinh} MLPs}

Given a single direction \(v\), Theorem~\ref{thm:rou} reduces the problem
to evaluating \(\Tp(x;v)\). We use forward-mode Taylor propagation
\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008}, replacing each intermediate activation
\(y\in\R^{B}\) by a length-\((p+1)\) tuple \((y_{0},\ldots,y_{p})\)
representing its truncated Taylor coefficients
\(y_{k}=(1/k!)\,d^{k}y/d\tau^{k}|_{\tau=0}\).

For an MLP \(u=\phi_{L}\circ A_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}\circ A_{1}\)
with affine \(A_{\ell}\) and entrywise activations \(\phi_{\ell}\), the
jet rules are as follows. The affine layer \(y=Wx+b\) propagates each
jet order independently:
\begin{equation}
y_{0}\;=\;W x_{0}+b,
\qquad y_{k}\;=\;W x_{k}\quad (k\ge 1).
\label{eq:jet-affine}
\end{equation}
The \(\tanh\) activation, with auxiliary jet \(z=1-y^{2}\), satisfies
\begin{equation}
y_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,z_{j}\,x_{k-j},
\qquad
z_{k}\;=\;-\sum_{j=0}^{k}y_{j}\,y_{k-j},
\qquad k\ge 1.
\label{eq:jet-tanh}
\end{equation}
The \(\sinh\) activation, with auxiliary jet \(z=\cosh(x)\), satisfies
the coupled recurrence
\begin{equation}
y_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,z_{j}\,x_{k-j},
\qquad
z_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)\,y_{j}\,x_{k-j},
\qquad k\ge 1.
\label{eq:jet-sinh}
\end{equation}
The \(\sinh\) recurrence is identical to that of \(\sin/\cos\) but
without the sign flip. These recurrences are algebraic identities and can
be evaluated on complex tensors. For the global complex-direction
backend used in the PDE experiment we use \(\sinh\), because
\(\sinh\) and \(\cosh\) are entire on \(\C\). The \(\tanh\) rule is kept
for real-valued experiments and local complex evaluations, but
\(\tanh\) is meromorphic and has poles in the complex plane.

Each Taylor coefficient \(y_{k}\) is a regular tensor, and the jet
propagation has no autograd-graph chaining, so storage is \((p+1)\)
times the ordinary forward-pass activation size. We additionally use a
custom \texttt{torch.autograd.Function} for each activation jet, with
the closed-form backward
\begin{equation}
g_{x}^{(m)}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{p-m}\overline{q_{j}}\,g_{y}^{(m+j)},
\qquad q\;=\;\phi',
\label{eq:jet-vjp}
\end{equation}
which avoids reverse differentiation through the intra-jet recurrence
and is essential for memory-efficient training.

\subsection{Backends}\label{ssec:backends}

We compare three implemented deterministic backends for evaluating one
fixed \(\dal u(x)\). All three are differentiable in the network
parameters \(\theta\), so each can be used directly as the residual term
of a PINN loss. The implementation also provides an \texttt{auto}
dispatch option, but it is a selection rule between B2 and B3 rather
than a separate mathematical backend.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(B1) Direct nested AD.}
The reference baseline performs \(p\) successive calls to PyTorch's
\texttt{autograd.grad}, each with retained computation graph. This
induces a reverse-mode autograd graph of depth \(O(pL)\) for a
depth-\(L\) network.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(B2) Real polarization with Taylor jet.}
The classical antipodally merged polarization identity is
\begin{equation}
\dal u(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{2^{p}}\sum_{\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{p}}
   \Big(\textstyle\prod_{r=1}^{p}\epsilon_{r}\Big)\,
   \Tp\!\Big(x;\,\textstyle\sum_{r=1}^{p}\epsilon_{r}\,e_{i_{r}}\Big).
\label{eq:polarization}
\end{equation}
After antipodal merging this yields at most \(2^{p-1}\) real directions.
The directional Taylor coefficients \(\Tp(x;\,\cdot\,)\) are evaluated
by the jet propagation of Section~\ref{sec:impl}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(B3) Complex Waring with Taylor jet.}
The roots-of-unity construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:rou} produces
\(R_{\C}(z^{\alpha})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}+1)\) directions in
\(\C^{n+1}\); for several repeated-index patterns this is smaller than
the coalesced polarization count of (B2). For real-parameter networks,
the complex
directions induce per-call casts of the linear weights via the
differentiable \(\mathtt{Tensor.to}\) operator; for complex-parameter
networks (Section~\ref{ssec:pinn}) no cast is required and the network
itself acts on the complex directions natively.

\subsection{The end-to-end algorithm}\label{ssec:algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:waring-jet} summarises the complete pipeline of
backend (B3): given a smooth model \(u\) (an MLP whose primitives are
covered by the jet rules \eqref{eq:jet-affine}--\eqref{eq:jet-sinh}), a
point \(x\), and a multi-index \(\alpha\) of order \(p\), it returns
the value \(\dal u(x)\) without any nested back-propagation.
Backend (B2) follows the same overall structure with the
Waring schedule (lines~3--7) replaced by the polarization schedule
\eqref{eq:polarization}.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Single-monomial \(\dal u(x)\) via complex Waring schedule + Taylor jet.}
\label{alg:waring-jet}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE smooth scalar field \(u\); point \(x\in\R^{d}\);
         multi-index \(\alpha\) of order \(p\)
\ENSURE  the value \(\dal u(x)\)
\STATE relabel the active coordinates of \(\alpha\) as
       \(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}\) with multiplicities
       \(1\le a_{0}\le\cdots\le a_{n}\)
\STATE \(m_{j}\leftarrow a_{j}+1\) for \(j=1,\ldots,n\); set
       \(\mathcal{I}\leftarrow U_{m_{1}}\!\times\cdots\times U_{m_{n}}\)
\STATE initialise schedule \(\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\emptyset\)
\FORALL{\(\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n})\in\mathcal{I}\)}
   \STATE \(v_{\zeta}\leftarrow e_{i_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}\,e_{i_{j}}\)
   \STATE \(c_{\zeta}\leftarrow
          \dfrac{\alpha!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}\)
   \STATE \(\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{S}\cup\{(c_{\zeta},v_{\zeta})\}\)
\ENDFOR
\STATE \(\mathrm{result}\leftarrow 0\)
\FORALL{\((c,v)\in\mathcal{S}\)}
   \STATE form the input jet
          \(\mathbf{J}_{0}\leftarrow(x,\,v,\,0,\,\ldots,\,0)\)
          of length \(p+1\)
   \STATE \(\mathbf{J}_{L}\leftarrow\) jet propagation of \(\mathbf{J}_{0}\)
          through every primitive of \(u\) using
          \eqref{eq:jet-affine}--\eqref{eq:jet-sinh}
   \STATE \(\Tp(x;v)\leftarrow [\mathbf{J}_{L}]_{p}\)
          \quad{\small$\triangleright$ order-\(p\) coefficient of the output jet}
   \STATE \(\mathrm{result}\leftarrow \mathrm{result}+c\cdot \Tp(x;v)\)
\ENDFOR
\RETURN \(\mathrm{result}\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:correctness}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} together with Theorem~\ref{thm:rou},
Algorithm~\ref{alg:waring-jet} returns \(\dal u(x)\) exactly (up to
floating-point round-off in the jet propagation).
The number of inner iterations is \(|\mathcal{S}|=R_{\C}(z^{\alpha})\),
which is information-theoretically optimal over \(\C\)
(Theorem~\ref{thm:CCG}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Cost and numerical considerations}

For a fixed direction, the Taylor jet stores \(p+1\) coefficient tensors
per activation and performs the same linear layers at each jet order;
there is no chain of nested reverse-mode differentiation through the
input coordinates. The total value-evaluation cost of B3 is therefore
proportional to \(R_{\C}(z^{\alpha})\) directional jet evaluations, while
B2 scales with the coalesced polarization count \(R_{\mathrm{pol}}\).
The rank reduction is an algebraic cost reduction; the realized
wall-clock time also depends on complex arithmetic, batching,
activation kernels, and whether parameter gradients are required.

The schedule coefficients are roots of unity multiplied by
\(\alpha!/R_{\C}\), so coefficient magnitudes are explicit and do not
come from solving an ill-conditioned linear system. The method has no
finite-difference step size and no Monte Carlo variance. Remaining
errors are ordinary floating-point round-off from Taylor recurrences,
complex arithmetic, and the final summation over directions.

\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% =========================================================

We evaluate three claims:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item[(C1)] The Waring + Taylor-jet backend matches direct nested AD to
  round-off accuracy on the value of \(\dal u(x)\), and supports
  parameter-gradient back-propagation in PINN training.
\item[(C2)] For repeated-index patterns where \(R_{\C}\) is strictly
  smaller than the coalesced polarization count, the complex-Waring
  backend uses fewer Taylor directions; measured wall-clock differences
  are reported rather than inferred solely from rank.
\item[(C3)] On a 4D manufactured sixth-order PDE, training a
  complex-parameter \(\sinh\) PINN with the Waring backend has a much
  smaller time-per-step than nested AD and reaches the lowest error in
  the single-seed fixed-budget run reported here.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental setup}

\textbf{Hardware.} Single-GPU NVIDIA T4 (16~GB), CUDA 12.1, PyTorch 2.5.

\textbf{Networks.} The direct-derivative microbenchmark uses a real
Linear--\(\sinh\) MLP with hidden width 64 and depth 4, matching the
saved run in \path{results/quick_compare_T4_20260602_0303.txt}.
The PDE experiment uses a complex-parameter Linear--\(\sinh\) MLP with
hidden width 32 and depth 4. In the PDE loss, the physical solution is
\(\Re u\); imaginary parts of the weights are regularized via a Tikhonov
term \(\lambda\|\mathrm{Im}\,W\|^{2}\) with \(\lambda=10^{-6}\).

\textbf{Precision and reference.} The microbenchmark runs in fp64, while
the PDE training uses complex128 parameters and activations. Reference
values for derivative accuracy are computed with backend B1 (direct
nested AD) on the same network instance.

\textbf{Timing protocol.} The microbenchmark reports median wall-clock
per derivative call over 60 timed repetitions after 5 warm-up calls, as
recorded in the saved result file. The PDE experiment gives each
backend a 60-second wall-clock training budget and reports the realized
per-step time and peak allocated GPU memory. The saved PDE run uses one

\subsection{PINN experiment: 4D manufactured sixth-order PDE}
\label{ssec:pinn}

\paragraph{Problem.}
Consider the manufactured PDE on \(\Omega=(-1,1)^{4}\):
\begin{equation}
\partial_{x_{1}}^{4}\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}u(x) \;=\; f(x),
\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega}\text{ given},
\label{eq:CH-pde}
\end{equation}
with \(u_{\mathrm{exact}}(x)=\sinh(x_{1})\cos(x_{2})\,
\mathrm{e}^{-(x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2})/4}\). Since
\(\sinh^{(4)}=\sinh\) and \(\cos^{(2)}=-\cos\), the source has the
closed form \(f=-u_{\mathrm{exact}}\). The dominant operator is the
single multi-index with active-exponent pattern \((4,2)\); by
Theorem~\ref{thm:CCG}, \(R_{\C}=5\) versus polarization count \(7\).

\paragraph{Architecture and training.}
We use the complex network specified in the experimental setup
(hidden width 32, depth 4) with \(d=4\), input embedded as
\(x\mapsto x+0\,\mathrm{i}\). The PINN loss combines an interior
residual term, a boundary-fit term, and a Tikhonov regularizer on the
imaginary part of the weights:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}
   \;+\;\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}
   \;+\;\lambda\,\sum_{W}\|\Im W\|^{2},
\label{eq:pinn-loss}
\end{equation}
with
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}\;=\;
   \E_{x\sim U(\Omega)}\big|\Re\partial^{(4,2)}u(x)-f(x)\big|^{2},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}\;=\;
   \E_{x\sim U(\partial\Omega)}\big|\Re u(x)-u_{\mathrm{exact}}(x)\big|^{2},
\]
\(\beta=10^{2}\) and \(\lambda=10^{-6}\). Training uses Adam with
learning rate \(10^{-3}\); each step samples
\(N_{\mathrm{int}}=128\) interior points and \(N_{\mathrm{bc}}=64\)
boundary points.

\paragraph{Comparison protocol.}
Each backend is given the same wall-clock budget of 60 seconds. We
report \emph{steps completed within the budget}, the wall-clock per
step, peak GPU memory, the final value of \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}\)
(averaged over the last 20 steps), and the relative \(L^{2}\) error of
\(\Re u\) against \(u_{\mathrm{exact}}\) on a held-out grid of size
\(4096\). Results in Table~\ref{tab:pinn-60s} are measured directly on
the T4 with the implementation of this paper. For a submission-quality
accuracy comparison between B2 and B3, the same protocol should be
repeated over multiple seeds with a fixed and recorded held-out
evaluation set.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =========================================================

\paragraph{Strengths.}
Complex Waring is at its strongest when the active-exponent pattern is
repeated and balanced, e.g., \((2,2)\), \((2,2,2)\), \((3,3)\),
\((4,2)\). For these the rank \(\prod_{j\ge 1}(a_{j}+1)\) is much smaller
than \(2^{p-1}\); concretely, \((2,2,2)\) yields rank 9 while polarization
needs 13 directions for the same multi-index. PINNs whose residual
contains such a single dominant operator -- not uncommon in fourth- and
sixth-order beam, plate, and Cahn--Hilliard models -- benefit directly.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Square-free patterns saturate the \(2^{p-1}\) bound over both \(\R\) and
\(\C\); for
\(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\partial_{z}\partial_{w}\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\)
the method offers no rank advantage over polarization. Operators that are
\emph{sums} of multi-indices (Laplacian, biharmonic) fall outside our
scope and are best served by trace-collapsing methods
\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian,Gao2023folx}.

\paragraph{Experimental evidence.}
The direct-derivative benchmark is a value-mode accuracy and timing test;
it verifies round-off agreement and exposes the direction-count
advantage, but it does not by itself prove a universal wall-clock ordering
between complex Waring and real polarization. The PINN experiment is a
single-seed fixed-budget run. It demonstrates that the jet backends reduce
per-step cost substantially relative to nested AD on the manufactured
problem, while the observed accuracy gap between B3 and B2 should be
validated with multiple seeds, fixed evaluation grids, and structured
CSV/JSON result files before being used as a statistical comparison.

\paragraph{Complex value fields on oscillatory PDEs.}
The oscillatory study (Section~\ref{sec:osc})
gives a second, independent reason to value the entire-on-\(\C\) backend: it
makes complex-parameter \(\sinh\) networks usable as a PINN ansatz, and that
ansatz is empirically more expressive than strong real high-frequency baselines
on most of a literature-grounded suite. The advantage is real but bounded. It
is largest exactly where the theory predicts -- balanced repeated-index
high-order operators, where the complex schedule is also rank-optimal -- and on
genuinely complex-valued fields, where a split real--imaginary surrogate is at a
structural disadvantage. It shrinks or disappears on first-order-in-time
dispersive operators (KdV) and on low-frequency heterogeneous media, and no
representation escapes the budget-limited saturation of high-frequency PINNs at
the most extreme orders and wavenumbers. We therefore present complex
\(\sinh\) networks as a competitive default for high-order and complex-valued
oscillatory PINNs, not as a universal replacement for real architectures.

\paragraph{Connection to STDE.}
The Stochastic Taylor Derivative Estimator \cite{Shi2024stde} solves a
strictly broader problem -- arbitrary contraction of the order-\(k\)
derivative tensor -- via stochastic sparse jets driven by integer
partitions. For one fixed multi-index \(\alpha\) our deterministic Waring
schedule is exact, while STDE is an unbiased estimator with variance
dictated by its sample budget. The two are complementary: STDE is the
natural choice when the operator is a linear combination of many
multi-indices; Waring is the natural choice when attention is on one
dominant multi-index that one wishes to evaluate exactly.

\section{Conclusion}
% =========================================================

We have shown that the construction of a directional schedule for one
mixed partial derivative \(\dal u\) of order \(p\) is exactly the problem
of a Waring decomposition of the monomial \(z^{\alpha}\). The classical
Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita rank theorem and its roots-of-unity
construction yield an explicit, rank-optimal directional schedule over
\(\C\); paired with Taylor-mode propagation through \(\sinh\)-activated
MLPs, this gives a deterministic, exact, parameter-differentiable
backend for high-order single-monomial residuals. In the tested 4D
manufactured sixth-order PDE, the jet backends are much faster per step
than nested automatic differentiation, and the complex Waring backend
achieves the lowest error in the reported single-seed fixed-budget run.

The method is at its most useful for repeated-index multi-indices, where
the complex Waring rank is materially smaller than the coalesced real
polarization count. For square-free patterns it offers no rank advantage;
for operator sums the rank is not shortened, although the jet still
evaluates them exactly term by term. Within its niche -- a single,
dominating, repeated-index high-order operator -- it provides an exact
deterministic alternative to stochastic estimators, while leaving broader
operator contractions to methods designed for that setting.

The same entire-on-\(\C\) backend has a second payoff. Because it makes
complex-parameter \(\sinh\) networks high-order differentiable at no extra
inner-loop cost, it enables a complex value field as a PINN ansatz. On a
literature-grounded suite of nine oscillatory and high-order PDE families,
evaluated at matched real-parameter counts and under one shared training
protocol against Fourier-feature, SIREN, and MscaleDNN baselines, the complex
\(\sinh\) network is the most accurate method on the majority of instances and
is clearly strongest on balanced high-order operators and on genuinely
complex-valued problems. It is not a universal win -- dispersive and
low-frequency heterogeneous problems remain better served by real
high-frequency networks, and all methods saturate at the most extreme
frequencies within a fixed budget -- but it is a competitive default precisely
in the high-order and complex-valued regimes that motivate this work.

\section{Proofs of Directional Schedule Identities}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Roots-of-unity filter}\label{app:rou-filter}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rou-filter}
For any positive integer \(m\) and integer \(k\),
\(\sum_{\zeta:\,\zeta^{m}=1}\zeta^{k}=m\,\mathbf{1}_{\{m\mid k\}}\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If \(m\mid k\) every term is \(1\); otherwise the sum is a non-trivial
geometric series in the primitive \(m\)-th root of unity, which sums to
zero.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rou}}\label{app:rou-proof}
We verify condition (2) of Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}: namely that the
schedule \eqref{eq:rou-coeff} satisfies
\(p!\,z^{\alpha}=\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}(v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\) in
\(\C[z]_{p}\).

Expanding \((v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}\) by the multinomial theorem and
collecting the coefficient of \(z^{\beta}\) (with \(|\beta|=p\)):
\[
[z^{\beta}]\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}(v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}
=\frac{\alpha!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}}\binom{p}{\beta}
 \prod_{j=1}^{n}\!\Big(\sum_{\zeta_{j}^{m_{j}}=1}\zeta_{j}^{1+\beta_{j}}\Big).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:rou-filter}, each inner sum equals \(m_{j}\) iff
\(m_{j}\mid 1+\beta_{j}\), i.e.\ \(\beta_{j}\equiv a_{j}\pmod{m_{j}}\),
and equals zero otherwise.

A short counting argument shows that the unique feasible joint assignment
is \(\beta=\alpha\). Indeed, the congruence forces
\(\beta_{j}=a_{j}+t_{j}(a_{j}+1)\) for \(j\ge 1\) with
\(t_{j}\ge 0\). If some \(t_{j}>0\), then
\[
\beta_{0}
=a_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}(a_{j}+1)
<0,
\]
because \(a_{j}\ge a_{0}\), impossible. Hence all \(t_{j}=0\) and
\(\beta=\alpha\). With \(\beta=\alpha\) all the \(m_{j}\) factors cancel
and the leading constant becomes
\(\alpha!\binom{p}{\alpha}=p!\), giving
\([z^{\alpha}]\sum_{\zeta}c_{\zeta}(v_{\zeta}\!\cdot\!z)^{p}=p!\) and
all other coefficients vanish. \qed

\subsection{Apolar pairing alternative}\label{app:apolar-pairing}
The Waring rank \(R_{\C}(F)\) of a homogeneous polynomial \(F\in\C[z]_{p}\)
admits the apolar characterization
\(R_{\C}(F)=\min\{\deg X:X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}\text{ a finite reduced
scheme apolar to }F\}\), where \(X\) is apolar to \(F\) iff
\(I_{X}\subset F^{\perp}\) and \(F^{\perp}\) is the apolar (Macaulay
inverse) ideal of \(F\). For \(F=z^{\alpha}\),
\(F^{\perp}=(\partial_{0}^{a_{0}+1},\ldots,\partial_{n}^{a_{n}+1})\),
which is a complete intersection. The Hilbert function of the quotient
\(\C[\partial]/F^{\perp}\) gives the lower bound matching
\eqref{eq:CCG-rank}; details in \cite{IK1999,Landsberg2012}.
