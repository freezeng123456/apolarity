%% ========== Abstract ==========
\begin{abstract}
  Computing high-order derivatives in neural network residuals is often
  computationally and memory intensive, primarily due to the huge computational
  graphs caused by automatic differentiation. In this work, we decompose the
  derivative into a weighted sum of directional Taylor coefficients. We prove
  that the minimal number of directional evaluations required to recover a
  target partial derivative equals the Waring rank of its corresponding
  monomial, and we attain this minimum with a roots-of-unity formula. Experiments
  show that our method significantly outperforms nested automatic
  differentiation in both speed and memory efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
high-order derivatives,
Taylor-mode automatic differentiation,
Waring decomposition,
directional Taylor coefficients
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec_intro}

Physics-informed neural networks approximate solutions of differential
equations by minimizing loss functions constructed from the corresponding PDE
residuals. This requires derivatives of the network with respect to its inputs.
For high-order equations, the repeated evaluation of these derivatives can
dominate both the runtime and memory cost of residual evaluation.

Automatic differentiation is the standard tool for evaluating these
derivatives~\cite{Griewank2008,Baydin2018,Paszke2019}. Several exact approaches
reduce its cost by exploiting the structure of the differential operator. For
example, forward Laplacian methods propagate the Laplacian through the network
without constructing the full Hessian~\cite{Li2024forwardLaplacian}. Such
methods can be efficient, but their differentiation rules are designed for
specific structured operators and do not readily extend to general high-order
partial derivatives. Taylor-mode automatic differentiation provides a more
general alternative by propagating a truncated univariate Taylor series through
the network~\cite{Bettencourt2019,Griewank2008}. Existing general constructions
recover multivariate high-order derivatives from multiple Taylor expansions,
often by reconstructing the full derivative tensor~\cite{GriewankUtkeWalther2000}.
The number of tensor components grows rapidly with both the input dimension and
the derivative order, resulting in substantial computational and GPU-memory
costs~\cite{Shi2024stde}.

Randomization offers another route to reducing derivative costs. Stochastic
dimension gradient descent uses coordinate sampling to evaluate only a subset
of derivative terms during each optimization step~\cite{Hu2023sdgd}.
Hutchinson-type estimators use random projections to approximate trace-type
differential operators~\cite{Hutchinson1989,Pearlmutter1994,Hu2024hte}.
However, both approaches still rely on automatic differentiation to compute the
sampled derivatives or projected vector products. A separate line of work
avoids automatic differentiation entirely when computing derivatives. Finite
differences approximate derivatives from nearby network
evaluations~\cite{Fornberg1988,Chiu2022CAN}, whereas randomized smoothing uses
Stein's identity to express derivatives of a smoothed network as expectations
of perturbed function values~\cite{Stein1981,He2023}. STDE takes a different
route by combining randomization with high-order automatic differentiation: it
constructs randomized input tangents so that general linear differential
operators can be estimated through univariate Taylor-mode
propagation~\cite{Shi2024stde}. STDE++ also discusses the exact recovery of
mixed partial derivatives from linear
combinations of directional Taylor coefficients. It further develops STDE by
providing polynomial-time Taylor-jet constructions, improving on earlier
general schemes whose cost grows exponentially with the derivative
order~\cite{Shi2026stdepp}. Although these methods reduce computational costs,
their performance depends on the choice of method-specific parameters. Finite
differences require a step size that balances truncation and round-off errors,
randomized smoothing depends on both the smoothing bandwidth and the Monte
Carlo sample size, and the randomized estimators require a sampling distribution
and budget. For the stochastic methods, these choices directly affect estimator
variance: insufficient or poorly matched sampling can yield high-variance
residual estimates, while a small smoothing bandwidth can sharply amplify
variance for high-order derivatives and may destabilize PINN
training~\cite{He2023,Hu2025bias,Shi2026stdepp}.

In this paper, we propose an exact method for recovering a partial derivative
as a linear combination of directional Taylor coefficients with the minimum
possible number of directions. Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemindent=0pt, labelsep=0.5em,
    itemsep=0.35em, topsep=0.45em, parsep=0pt]
\item We establish an equivalence between Waring decompositions of a monomial
and decompositions of the corresponding partial derivative into directional
Taylor coefficients. The equivalence shows that the minimum number of
directions equals the Waring rank of the monomial.
\item We derive an explicit roots-of-unity formula attaining the minimum number
of directions. A batched Taylor-mode implementation evaluates all directional
Taylor coefficients independently and in parallel.
\item We compare the proposed method with nested automatic differentiation on
manufactured PINN benchmarks. The experiments show reductions in runtime and
peak memory, with larger reductions at higher derivative orders.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec_prelim}
introduces the method of PINNs, the multi-index notation, and the directional
Taylor coefficient.
Section~\ref{sec_formulation} proves the equivalence with monomial Waring
rank and derives the rank-optimal formula. Section~\ref{sec_implementation}
describes its realization with batched Taylor jets. Section~\ref{sec_experiments}
compares the implementation with backpropagation applied $p$ times on
manufactured residuals.
Section~\ref{sec_concl} concludes. The roots-of-unity formula, the
schedules used in the experiments, and the argument for the rank bound are
collected in Appendix~\ref{sec_schedules}.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec_prelim}

\subsection{Method of PINNs}\label{sec_pinn}

Let $\Omega \subset \R^{d}$ be a computational domain with boundary
$\Gamma := \partial\Omega$. We consider the boundary-value problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq_pde}
    \mathcal{P} u_{\star} = f \quad\text{in } \Omega,
    \qquad
    \mathcal{B} u_{\star} = g \quad\text{on } \Gamma,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}$ is a differential operator and $\mathcal{B}$ is a
boundary operator or a collection of boundary operators. In the PINN
framework, $u_{\star}$ is approximated by a neural network
$u_{\theta}$~\cite{Lagaris1998,Raissi2019,Sirignano2018,Karniadakis2021}.
Let $(\bx_{r,i})_{i=1}^{n_r}\subset\Omega$ and
$(\bx_{b,i})_{i=1}^{n_b}\subset\Gamma$ denote the residual and boundary
points. For weights $\lambda_r,\lambda_b>0$, the network parameters are
obtained by minimizing
\begin{equation}\label{eq_pinn_loss}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta)
    :=
    \frac{\lambda_r}{n_r}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n_r}
    \abs{\mathcal{P}u_{\theta}(\bx_{r,i})-f(\bx_{r,i})}^{2}
    +
    \frac{\lambda_b}{n_b}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n_b}
    \norm{\mathcal{B}u_{\theta}(\bx_{b,i})-g(\bx_{b,i})}^{2}.
\end{equation}
Evaluating the PDE residual in \eqref{eq_pinn_loss} requires derivatives of
$u_{\theta}$. For high-order equations, nested automatic differentiation can
dominate the time and memory required for residual evaluation because it
retains large intermediate computational graphs and repeatedly differentiates
through the network.

\subsection{Multi-index notation and the target derivative}\label{sec_problem}

Let $\N_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, and let $\C^d$ denote the
$d$-dimensional complex space. For $i=1,\ldots,d$, define
$\partial_i:=\partial/\partial x_i$. Let $\nu\in\N_0^d$ be a multi-index,
and let $\bz\in\C^d$. With $\nu=(\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_d)$ and
$\bz=(z_1,\ldots,z_d)$, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq_multiindex}
    \abs{\nu}
    :=
    \sum_{i=1}^{d}\nu_i,
    \qquad
    \nu!
    :=
    \prod_{i=1}^{d}\nu_i!,
    \qquad
    \bz^{\nu}
    :=
    \prod_{i=1}^{d}z_i^{\nu_i},
    \qquad
    \partial^{\nu}
    :=
    \partial_1^{\nu_1}\cdots\partial_d^{\nu_d}.
\end{equation}
The order of $\partial^\nu$ is $p:=\abs{\nu}$.

We consider one target partial derivative at a time. Fix a point
$\bx\in\Omega$ and a multi-index $\nu$ of order $p\geq1$. For the analysis
below, let $u$ denote the scalar function being differentiated; in the PINN
setting, $u=u_{\theta}$. The target derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{eq_target_partial}
    \dal u(\bx)
    :=
    \partial^{\nu}u(\bx).
\end{equation}

We assume that $u$ admits a holomorphic extension to a complex neighborhood
of $\bx$ and use the same notation for the extension. Feed-forward networks
composed of affine maps and entire activations, such as
$\sinh(z):=(e^{z}-e^{-z})/2$, admit such an extension. The assumption covers
networks whose restriction to $\R^d$ is real-valued as well as networks used
for complex-valued solutions.

\subsection{Directional Taylor coefficients}\label{sec_taylor}

For $\bv\in\C^d$, define the order-$p$ Taylor coefficient of $u$ at $\bx$
along $\bv$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq_tp}
    \Tp(\bx;\bv)
    :=
    \frac{1}{p!}
    \frac{d^p}{d\tau^p}
    u(\bx+\tau\bv)\Big|_{\tau=0},
\end{equation}
Thus, $\Tp(\bx;\bv)$ is the coefficient of $\tau^p$ in the univariate
Taylor expansion of $u(\bx+\tau\bv)$. The chain rule for the affine map
$\tau\mapsto\bx+\tau\bv$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq_tp_frechet}
    \Tp(\bx;\bv)
    =
    \frac{1}{p!}D^p u(\bx)[\bv,\ldots,\bv],
\end{equation}
where $D^p u(\bx)$ denotes the symmetric complex $p$-linear derivative of
$u$ at $\bx$.

\section{Exact directional representations and Waring rank}\label{sec_formulation}

Fix $\bx$ and $\nu$ as in Section~\ref{sec_problem}, and set $p=\abs{\nu}$.
After permuting the coordinates, write
\[
    \nu=(a_0,\ldots,a_n,0,\ldots,0),
\]
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq_reduced_multiindex}
    1\leq a_0\leq\cdots\leq a_n,
    \qquad
    p=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_j,
    \qquad
    \nu!=\prod_{j=0}^{n}a_j!.
\end{equation}
Let $e_0,\ldots,e_n$ denote the standard basis vectors corresponding to the
nonzero entries of $\nu$, and write $\bz^a:=z_0^{a_0}\cdots z_n^{a_n}$.

\subsection{Directional representations and Waring decompositions}
\label{sec_expansion}

For a direction $\bv=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_je_j$, the multinomial theorem and
\eqref{eq_tp_frechet} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq_directional_expansion}
    \Tp(\bx;\bv)
    =
    \sum_{\substack{\beta\in\N_0^{n+1}\\\abs{\beta}=p}}
    \frac{\bv^\beta}{\beta!}\,
    \partial^\beta u(\bx).
\end{equation}
Thus each directional Taylor coefficient is a linear combination of the
order-$p$ partial derivatives. An \emph{$R$-term exact directional
representation} of $\partial^\nu u(\bx)$ is an identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq_schedule}
    \partial^\nu u(\bx)
    =
    \sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r\Tp(\bx;\bv_r)
\end{equation}
that holds for every $u$ holomorphic on a complex neighborhood of $\bx$, where
$c_r\in\C$ and $\bv_r\in\spanop\{e_0,\ldots,e_n\}$.

\begin{proposition}[Directional--Waring correspondence]\label{prop_bridge}
    The coefficients and directions in \eqref{eq_schedule} form an exact
    directional representation if and only if
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_bridge}
        p!\,\bz^a
        =
        \sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r(\bv_r\cdot\bz)^p
        \qquad\text{in }\C[z_0,\ldots,z_n].
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Substituting \eqref{eq_directional_expansion} into \eqref{eq_schedule}, the
    coefficient multiplying $\partial^\beta u(\bx)$ on the right-hand side is
    $\beta!^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r\bv_r^\beta$. Testing the identity on
    degree-$p$ polynomials in the shifted coordinates shows that these
    coefficients must agree separately. Hence \eqref{eq_schedule} holds for
    every such $u$ if and only if
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_moment}
        \sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r\bv_r^\beta
        =
        \nu!\,\delta_{a\beta},
        \qquad \abs{\beta}=p,
    \end{equation}
    where $\delta_{a\beta}=1$ for $\beta=a$ and is zero otherwise. The
    multinomial theorem also gives
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r(\bv_r\cdot\bz)^p
        =
        \sum_{\abs{\beta}=p}
        \binom{p}{\beta}
        \left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r\bv_r^\beta\right)\bz^\beta.
    \end{equation*}
    The moment conditions \eqref{eq_moment} leave only the term $\beta=a$,
    whose coefficient is $\binom{p}{a}\nu!=p!$. Conversely, comparison of the
    coefficients of $\bz^\beta$ in \eqref{eq_bridge} recovers
    \eqref{eq_moment}.
\end{proof}

Restricting the directions to $\spanop\{e_0,\ldots,e_n\}$ does not change the
minimum. An exact representation using directions in the ambient space gives a
Waring identity for the target monomial in all $d$ variables. Setting all other
polynomial variables to zero gives an identity in $z_0,\ldots,z_n$ without
increasing the number of summands.

Equation~\eqref{eq_bridge} is a Waring decomposition of the scalar multiple
$p!\bz^a$. We recall the corresponding rank.

\begin{definition}[Waring rank]\label{def_waring}
    Let $F\in\C[z_0,\ldots,z_n]$ be homogeneous of degree $p$. The
    \emph{complex Waring rank} $R_{\C}(F)$ is the least integer $R$ for which
    there exist coefficients $c_r\in\C$ and linear forms $L_r$ satisfying
    \begin{equation*}
        F=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_rL_r^p.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

Multiplication by a nonzero scalar does not change Waring rank. Therefore,
Proposition~\ref{prop_bridge} identifies the minimum number of directions in
\eqref{eq_schedule} with $R_{\C}(\bz^a)$. The monomial rank theorem supplies
the minimum, while the construction below specifies directions and weights
that attain it. For $m\geq1$, let
$U_m:=\{\zeta\in\C:\zeta^m=1\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Rank-optimal directional representation]\label{thm_main}
    Let $1\leq a_0\leq\cdots\leq a_n$ be the nonzero entries of $\nu$ in
    \eqref{eq_reduced_multiindex}. The minimum number of terms in an exact
    directional representation of $\partial^\nu u(\bx)$ is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_rank}
        R_{\C}(\bz^a)
        =
        \prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_j+1).
    \end{equation}
    For
    $\zeta=(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_n)\in
    U_{a_1+1}\times\cdots\times U_{a_n+1}$, define
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_rou}
        \bv_\zeta
        :=
        e_0+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_je_j,
        \qquad
        c_\zeta
        :=
        \frac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_j+1)}
        \prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_j.
    \end{equation}
    Then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_optimal_representation}
        \partial^\nu u(\bx)
        =
        \sum_{\zeta\in U_{a_1+1}\times\cdots\times U_{a_n+1}}
        c_\zeta\Tp(\bx;\bv_\zeta).
    \end{equation}
    The representation \eqref{eq_optimal_representation} attains the minimum
    in \eqref{eq_rank}, even when directions in the ambient space $\C^d$ are
    allowed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Proposition~\ref{prop_bridge}, invariance of Waring rank under nonzero
    scaling, and the preceding projection argument identify the minimum over
    ambient directions with $R_{\C}(\bz^a)$. The monomial rank theorem of
    Carlini, Catalisano, and Geramita~\cite{CCG2012} gives \eqref{eq_rank}.
    Appendix~\ref{sec_apolar} records the apolar interpretation.

    It remains to verify that the roots-of-unity construction attains the
    minimum. For $\abs{\beta}=p$, summing over
    $\zeta\in U_{a_1+1}\times\cdots\times U_{a_n+1}$ gives
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_root_moments}
    \begin{aligned}
        \sum_{\zeta}c_\zeta\bv_\zeta^\beta
        &=
        \frac{\nu!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_j+1)}
        \prod_{j=1}^{n}
        \left(
            \sum_{\zeta_j\in U_{a_j+1}}
            \zeta_j^{\beta_j+1}
        \right).
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    The roots-of-unity filter in Lemma~\ref{lem_filter} shows that the $j$th
    factor is zero unless
    \begin{equation*}
        \beta_j\equiv a_j\pmod{a_j+1},
        \qquad j=1,\ldots,n.
    \end{equation*}
    Nonnegativity then implies that each $\beta_j$ is either $a_j$ or at least
    $2a_j+1$. If the second alternative holds for an index $j\geq1$, all
    filtered exponents satisfy $\beta_k\geq a_k$ for $k=1,\ldots,n$. The
    excess at the index $j$ is at least $a_j+1$. Therefore,
    \begin{equation*}
        \beta_0
        =
        p-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_k
        \leq
        a_0-(a_j+1)
        <0,
    \end{equation*}
    where the last inequality follows from $a_0\leq a_j$. This contradicts
    $\beta_0\geq0$. Consequently, the product in
    \eqref{eq_root_moments} can be nonzero only for $\beta=a$. For $\beta=a$,
    every factor equals $a_j+1$, and \eqref{eq_root_moments} equals $\nu!$.
    The moment conditions \eqref{eq_moment} therefore hold, which proves
    \eqref{eq_optimal_representation}. When $n=0$, the formula reduces to
    $\partial_0^p u(\bx)=p!\Tp(\bx;e_0)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Examples and consequences}\label{sec_consequences}

In the examples below, we return to the original coordinate labels. For the
mixed second derivative, $a=(1,1)$ and \eqref{eq_rank} gives rank two. Formula
\eqref{eq_optimal_representation} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq_two_point}
    \partial_1\partial_2u(\bx)
    =
    \frac{1}{2}\Tp(\bx;e_1+e_2)
    -
    \frac{1}{2}\Tp(\bx;e_1-e_2).
\end{equation}
Since $R_{\C}(z_0z_1)=2$, no exact representation of the mixed second
derivative by a single directional Taylor coefficient exists.

For a genuinely complex example, consider $a=(2,2)$ and let
$\omega=\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}/3)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq_example_22}
    \partial_1^2\partial_2^2u(\bx)
    =
    \frac{4}{3}
    \sum_{k=0}^{2}
    \omega^k\Tp(\bx;e_1+\omega^ke_2),
\end{equation}
and three directions are minimal over $\C$.

The rank formula also identifies the limits of the reduction. A pure power
$\partial_i^pu$ has rank one, whereas a square-free derivative with
$a=(1,\ldots,1)$ has rank $2^{p-1}$. Between these cases, repeated indices can
reduce the rank below the square-free value. Table~\ref{tab_patterns} lists
representative values, and Appendix~\ref{sec_lowschedules} records the formulas
used in the experiments.

The minimum in Theorem~\ref{thm_main} concerns one fixed monomial derivative
and exact linear representations by order-$p$ directional Taylor coefficients.
A differential operator containing several monomials is evaluated term by term.
By contrast, the stochastic Taylor derivative estimator~\cite{Shi2024stde}
uses a sampling budget to estimate arbitrary contractions of the order-$p$
derivative tensor. The stochastic estimation problem is distinct from the
deterministic minimum in \eqref{eq_rank}.

\section{Computational realization}\label{sec_implementation}

\subsection{Batched Taylor-jet evaluation}\label{sec_algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} assembles the pieces. The schedule \eqref{eq_rou} is
built once, its directions are stacked into a single batch, and one jet pass
through the function returns the order-$p$ coefficient for every direction at
once; the reported value is then the coefficient-weighted sum. The schedule
tensors depend on $\nu$, the device, and the scalar type, but not on $\theta$.
The reference implementation builds them at call entry and includes that work in
the measured throughput of Section~\ref{sec_experiments}, while a caller that
reuses one multi-index may cache them. For a real-valued function the conjugate
pairing of the roots of unity makes the returned value real in exact arithmetic;
the implementation keeps the complex tensor and takes its real part only when
the target PDE is real-valued.

The roots-of-unity directions are generally complex, so the network is
evaluated on complex Taylor jets. Entire activations such as $\sinh$ and
$\cosh$ are holomorphic on all of $\C$. By contrast, $\tanh$ is meromorphic,
with poles at $\mathrm{i}\pi/2+\mathrm{i}k\pi$ for $k\in\Z$, and is admissible
only when the relevant complex paths avoid those poles. The experiments use
$\sinh$ at every derivative order.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
  \caption{Single-monomial $\dal u(\bx)$ from a rank-optimal schedule and one batched Taylor jet.}
  \label{alg_waring}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \REQUIRE scalar network $u$ built from supported analytic primitives; point $\bx \in \R^{d}$; multi-index $\nu$ of order $p$
  \ENSURE the value $\dal u(\bx)$
  \STATE Relabel the coordinate vectors for the nonzero entries of $\nu$ as $e_0,\ldots,e_n$, with $1\leq a_0\leq\cdots\leq a_n$.
  \STATE \label{alg_line_first} Set $\mathcal{I} := U_{a_1+1}\times\cdots\times U_{a_n+1}$.
  \STATE Initialize $\mathcal{S} \gets \emptyset$.
  \FOR{$\zeta = (\zeta_{1}, \ldots, \zeta_{n}) \in \mathcal{I}$}
      \STATE Set $\bv_{\zeta} \gets e_0+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_je_j$ and $c_{\zeta} \gets \nu!\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_j+1)\bigr)^{-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_j$.
      \STATE Update $\mathcal{S} \gets \mathcal{S} \cup \{(c_{\zeta}, \bv_{\zeta})\}$.
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE \label{alg_line_last} Stack the directions of $\mathcal{S}$ into $V_{\mathcal{S}} \in \C^{\abs{\mathcal{S}} \times d}$.
  \STATE Form the batched input jet $\mathbf{J}_{0} \gets (\bx \text{ replicated}, V_{\mathcal{S}}, 0, \ldots, 0)$.
  \STATE Propagate $\mathbf{J}_{0}$ through $u$ by Taylor-mode automatic differentiation to obtain $\mathbf{J}_{L}$.
  \STATE Set $t \gets [\mathbf{J}_{L}]_{p}$, one order-$p$ coefficient per direction.
  \STATE \textbf{return} $\sum_{r=1}^{\abs{\mathcal{S}}} c_{r}t_{r}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Parameter gradients}\label{sec_gradients}

Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} must be differentiable in $\theta$, since its output
enters a residual assembled from monomial derivatives. Differentiating the
activation jets by reverse-mode automatic differentiation through an unrolled
recurrence would rebuild inside the backward pass the nesting that the jet
was introduced to avoid. We therefore register each activation jet as a single
primitive with the closed-form adjoint
\begin{equation}\label{eq_vjp}
    g_{x}^{(m)} = \sum_{j=0}^{p-m} \overline{q_{j}}\,g_{y}^{(m+j)}, \quad q(\tau) := \phi'\bigl(x(\tau)\bigr),
\end{equation}
in which $q_{j}$ is the $j$th Taylor coefficient of $q$ and $g^{(m)}$ denotes
the cotangent of the order-$m$ coefficient. Equation~\eqref{eq_vjp} follows from
the chain rule applied to the jet of $\phi$ and uses the convention of a
real-valued loss for complex parameters, so that the conjugation is the
Hermitian adjoint of multiplication by $q_{j}$. The backward pass thus costs one
more triangular convolution per activation and never differentiates through the
recurrence itself.

\subsection{Computational cost}\label{sec_cost}

The cost of Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} separates into a per-direction and a
per-schedule factor. For one direction, the jet stores $p+1$ coefficient tensors
per activation, an affine layer applies $W$ to each of them, and the activation
recurrences require $\sum_{k=1}^{p}O(k) = O(p^{2})$ elementwise products per
scalar. The total work and the directional storage then scale linearly in the
schedule length $R_{\C}(\bz^{a})$, which by Theorem~\ref{thm_main} is as small
as an exact directional formula can be. The rank reduction is an exact statement
about that factor and not about wall-clock time, which also depends on complex
arithmetic, kernel launch overhead, memory traffic, and whether parameter
gradients are required; for small widths or small batches these terms can
dominate. Section~\ref{sec_experiments} therefore reports measured throughput
alongside accuracy.

The schedule coefficients are known in closed form. The remaining error is
floating-point round-off from the Taylor recurrences, from complex arithmetic,
and from the final summation over directions. Section~\ref{sec_num_verify}
reports double-precision agreement through order six.

\section{Numerical experiments}\label{sec_experiments}

The experiments evaluate monomials that appear in the PINN residual
\eqref{eq_pinn_loss} for the boundary-value problem \eqref{eq_pde}. The
benchmark source terms and boundary data are manufactured from prescribed exact
solutions~\cite{Roache2002}.
Expanding $\mathcal{P}$ into monomials turns one residual evaluation in
\eqref{eq_pinn_loss} into several derivatives $\partial^{\alpha}u_{\theta}$ of
the same function on the same batch. The operator $\Delta^{m}$ expands into
$\binom{d+m-1}{m}$ distinct monomials: the pure terms $\partial_{x_{i}}^{2m}$
and, for $m \ge 2$, mixed terms such as
$\partial_{x_{i}}^{2k}\partial_{x_{j}}^{2m-2k}$.

This section checks two properties of the schedule: agreement with nested
automatic differentiation in double precision, and the time and memory of one
residual evaluation, including the reverse-mode pass. The first comparison uses
no PDE. The second repeats the same evaluation inside an otherwise identical
PINN training loop and records the time and memory of one step. Every quantity
below is a property of the derivative method on a fixed function, residual,
and optimizer.

\subsection{Verification of the derivative backend}\label{sec_num_verify}

All checks use double precision and are executable tests in the repository. The
roots-of-unity coefficient filter of Lemma~\ref{lem_filter} is evaluated against
every degree-four monomial in five variables for the pattern of nonzero exponents
$(2,1,1)$, with a maximum admissible absolute coefficient error of $10^{-12}$.
The length of the schedule that the implementation builds is checked against the
closed-form rank \eqref{eq_rank} for every pattern of Table~\ref{tab_patterns}.

The Waring jet of Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring} is then compared with nested
automatic differentiation on real-parameter networks using the $\tanh$,
logistic, $\sin$, and $\sinh$ activations, for the patterns $(2,2)$,
$(1,1,1)$, and $(3)$. Values must agree to relative tolerance
$2 \times 10^{-10}$ and absolute tolerance $2 \times 10^{-11}$, and the
imaginary remainder of a real target must not exceed $2 \times 10^{-11}$.
Parameter gradients of a squared derivative loss are compared at tolerances
$2 \times 10^{-9}$ relative and $2 \times 10^{-11}$ absolute, on a real
$\tanh$ network and on a complex $\sinh$ network; additional analytic checks
cover the orders $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, and $6$ for a scalar complex $\sinh$
network. Every check passes at the tolerance stated for it. These tests
establish that the implementation computes what Theorem~\ref{thm_main}
predicts.

\subsection{A controlled comparison of derivative backends}\label{sec_num_protocol}

The remaining study isolates the derivative backend. Protocol \texttt{jsc\_v3}
runs two lines that share the network, the loss, the optimizer, the residual
points, the evaluation rule, the seeds, and the wall-clock budget, and differ in
one respect: how each monomial derivative of the residual is evaluated. The
first line uses Algorithm~\ref{alg_waring}, that is, the rank-optimal complex
schedule evaluated by one batched Taylor jet, and is labeled \emph{jet} below.
The second evaluates the same derivative of the same network by nested
reverse-mode automatic differentiation, with the graph retained after every
coordinate derivative, and is labeled \emph{nested}. Both lines are the same
complex-parameter $\sinh$ network with four hidden layers of width $H = 128$,
hence the same 100{,}098 trainable real degrees of freedom, the same first-layer
scale, and the same \texttt{complex128} arithmetic. Both use Adam with initial
learning rate $10^{-3}$ and cosine decay, 4096 fixed interior points, 512 fixed
boundary points, paired point sets for each seed, the same per-setting boundary
weights, and a common held-out set of 8192 points. Each line is run for three
seeds with a budget of 1000 seconds per run.

A \emph{setting} is one problem together with one value of its sweep parameter,
and the protocol covers nine: the two-dimensional polyharmonic problem at orders
$2m \in \{2,4,6\}$, the radial chirp at $a \in \{1,2,3\}$, and the time-harmonic
Maxwell problem at $a \in \{2,4,6\}$, all defined as in
Section~\ref{sec_num_problems}. All 54 runs were produced by one source commit
on one NVIDIA H20 GPU with PyTorch 2.5.1 and CUDA 12.1, so unlike a comparison
assembled from separate campaigns, timings here are comparable across settings
as well as within one. Cost is the measured time per optimizer step together
with the peak memory allocated by a run, and accuracy is the held-out relative
$L^{2}$ error
\begin{equation}\label{eq_rel_error}
    \mathcal{E}_{L^{2}} := \frac{\norm{u_{\theta}-u_{\star}}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\norm{u_{\star}}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}},
\end{equation}
evaluated on that held-out set against the exact solution $u_{\star}$. A setting
enters the tables only after automated checks confirm all six method-seed rows,
finite metrics, nonempty monotone histories, and the protocol metadata.

\subsection{Benchmark problems}\label{sec_num_problems}

The three families place different demands on the derivative. For the
polyharmonic problem~\cite{Gazzola2010,Ciarlet1997} on $(-1,1)^{2}$ we solve
\begin{equation}\label{eq_num_poly}
    \Delta^{m}u = (-2\pi^{2})^{m}u_{\star} \quad\text{in }(-1,1)^{2}, \quad u_{\star}(\bx) = \sin(\pi x_{1})\sin(\pi x_{2}),
\end{equation}
at the orders $2m \in \{2,4,6\}$, with the Navier boundary targets $u =
u_{\star}$ and $\Delta^{j}u = (-2\pi^{2})^{j}u_{\star}$ for $j = 1, \ldots,
m-1$. Those traces vanish on $\partial(-1,1)^{2}$ because $u_{\star}$ does.
This family is the reason the derivative order appears as a variable: expanding
$\Delta^{m}$ in two dimensions produces the patterns $(2m)$ and, for $m \ge 2$,
the mixed patterns whose schedules Table~\ref{tab_patterns} lists. At $2m = 4$
the mixed term is $\partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}$, which \eqref{eq_rou}
evaluates with three directions; at $2m = 6$ the mixed terms are
$\partial_{1}^{4}\partial_{2}^{2}$ and $\partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{4}$,
which take five each.

The other two families sit at order two and sweep a frequency instead. On
$(-1,1)^{2}$ the radial chirp~\cite{Ihlenburg1998} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq_num_chirp}
    -\Delta u + u = f, \quad u_{\star}(x,y) = \sin\Bigl(\frac{a\pi}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})\Bigr),
\end{equation}
with Dirichlet data from $u_{\star}$, $a \in \{1,2,3\}$, and the manufactured
source $f = (a\pi)^{2}r^{2}\sin\phi - 2a\pi\cos\phi + \sin\phi$, where $\phi :=
a\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})/2$ and $r^{2} := x^{2}+y^{2}$. Its local radial wavenumber
$\abs{\nabla\phi} = a\pi r$ varies over the domain, so the target is not a
separable Fourier mode. For a two-dimensional transverse-magnetic mode,
Maxwell's equations~\cite{Monk2003} reduce to a scalar complex Helmholtz
equation for the
out-of-plane field $E$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq_num_maxwell}
    \Delta E + \kappa^{2}E = f, \quad \kappa^{2} = (a\pi)^{2}(1+\mathrm{i}\beta), \quad E_{\star} = \exp\bigl(\mathrm{i}a\pi(x+y)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\beta = 0.2$, exact Dirichlet data, $a \in \{2,4,6\}$, and $f =
[-2(a\pi)^{2}+\kappa^{2}]E_{\star}$. The Maxwell family has a complex-valued
solution. The measurements already propagate complex tensors through a
complex-parameter $\sinh$ network, so a complex residual uses the same jet as
the real ones. Since both families are of order two, every monomial term of
their residuals is a pure power, which the schedule evaluates with a single
real direction. They therefore isolate the cost of the Taylor jet on one
direction.

them. The jet replaces a graph of depth proportional to the derivative order and
the network depth by $p+1$ coefficient tensors per activation, which is what the
memory column measures; this part is available at every order and is what the
order-two settings show. The schedule then fixes how many times that jet must be
run, at the minimum of Table~\ref{tab_patterns}: one direction for each pure
power at any order, three for the mixed term at order four, five for each mixed
term at order six. Nested differentiation has no comparable lever, since it must
traverse the retained graph once per coordinate derivative of every term, which
is why the ratio in panel (c) of Figure~\ref{fig_v3_cost} widens with the order.
Both counts are exact. Table~\ref{tab_v3_cost} shows that on this hardware the
reduction survives complex arithmetic, kernel launches, and the backward pass.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec_concl}

The shortest exact formula for a high-order derivative, in directional Taylor
coefficients of the same order, is the Waring decomposition of the corresponding
monomial. Carlini, Catalisano, and Geramita evaluate the complex length as
\eqref{eq_rank}, and a roots-of-unity formula attains it. No shorter linear
combination of order-$p$ directional Taylor coefficients exists; the two-point
identity for $\partial_{1}\partial_{2}$ is the length-two case.

Combined with a batched Taylor jet and a closed-form vector-Jacobian product,
the schedule evaluates the derivative exactly and remains differentiable in
$\theta$. Double-precision tests reproduce nested automatic differentiation for
values and for parameter gradients through order six. Measured against nested
differentiation, with the function, the loss, and the optimizer held fixed, the
schedule reduces the time of one evaluation by factors of about $1.4$, $2.3$,
and $4.1$
at derivative orders two, four, and six, and its peak memory by about $1.5$,
$3.1$, and $6.5$; on the higher-order problems the evaluations thus gained lower
the error reached within a fixed budget. The two mechanisms behind that growth
are separable in the measurements: Taylor-mode evaluation removes the nested
graph at every order, and the rank reduction shortens the schedule wherever an
index repeats.

The schedule is shortest for repeated-index multi-indices and exponentially long
for square-free ones: Theorem~\ref{thm_main} shows that this length is the
minimum. At low derivative order a cheaper derivative also buys little in a
complete run, since the derivative is not what limits it. Natural continuations
are a rank-aware expansion of an operator that reuses directions across its
terms, a conditioning analysis of the schedule at high order and in reduced
precision, and the extension of the same apolarity argument to derivative
tensors contracted against a fixed structured family.

\section{Schedules, filters, and the apolar bound}\label{sec_schedules}

This appendix collects the roots-of-unity filter used in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm_main}, the explicit schedules for the derivative patterns that
occur in Section~\ref{sec_experiments}, and the apolar interpretation of the
rank formula \eqref{eq_rank}. The formulas of Appendix~\ref{sec_lowschedules}
are those evaluated by the implementation.

\subsection{Roots-of-unity filter}\label{sec_filter}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem_filter}
    For every integer $m \ge 1$ and every integer $k$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_filter}
        \sum_{\zeta \in U_m}\zeta^k
        =
        \begin{cases}
            m, & m\mid k,\\
            0, & m\nmid k.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\omega := \exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}/m)$, so that every element of $U_{m}$ is
    $\omega^{\ell}$ for exactly one $\ell \in \{0, \ldots, m-1\}$ and
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{\zeta \in U_{m}} \zeta^{k} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\bigl(\omega^{k}\bigr)^{\ell}.
    \end{equation*}
    If $m \mid k$, then $\omega^{k} = 1$ and the sum equals $m$. If $m \nmid k$,
    then $\omega^{k} \ne 1$, and the finite geometric series gives
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\bigl(\omega^{k}\bigr)^{\ell} = \frac{1-\omega^{km}}{1-\omega^{k}} = 0,
    \end{equation*}
    since $\omega^{m} = 1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Explicit low-order schedules}\label{sec_lowschedules}

The mixed terms of the polyharmonic residual \eqref{eq_num_poly} and the pure
powers of the chirp and Maxwell residuals use the following instances of
\eqref{eq_rou}.

For a pure power $\dal = \partial_{1}^{p}$ we have $n = 0$, the schedule has one
direction, and $\Tp$ is evaluated once,
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_pure}
    \partial_{1}^{p}u(\bx) = p!\,\Tp(\bx; e_{1}).
\end{equation}

For the fourth-order mixed term $\dal = \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}$ we
have $n = 1$ and $a = (2,2)$; with $\omega :=
\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}/3)$ and $U_{3} = \{1, \omega, \omega^{2}\}$, formula
\eqref{eq_rou} gives the three directions
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_22}
    \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{2}u(\bx) = \frac{4}{3}\sum_{\zeta \in U_{3}} \zeta\,\Tp(\bx;\, e_{1}+\zeta e_{2}).
\end{equation}

For the sixth-order mixed term $\dal = \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{4}$ we
have $n = 1$ and $a = (2,4)$; with $U_{5}$ the fifth roots of
unity, \eqref{eq_rou} takes the coordinate of smaller exponent as base and
gives the five directions
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_24}
    \partial_{1}^{2}\partial_{2}^{4}u(\bx) = \frac{48}{5}\sum_{\zeta \in U_{5}} \zeta\,\Tp(\bx;\, e_{1}+\zeta e_{2}).
\end{equation}
The companion term $\partial_{1}^{4}\partial_{2}^{2}$ is the same formula with
the two coordinates exchanged. The equivalent polynomial identity
\eqref{eq_bridge} for \eqref{eq_sched_24} reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq_sched_24_poly}
    6!\,z_{0}^{2}z_{1}^{4} = \frac{48}{5}\sum_{\zeta \in U_{5}} \zeta\,(z_{0}+\zeta z_{1})^{6},
\end{equation}
and it can be checked directly: the coefficient of
$z_{0}^{\beta_{0}}z_{1}^{\beta_{1}}$ on the right-hand side contains the factor
$\sum_{\zeta \in U_{5}}\zeta^{1+\beta_{1}}$, which by Lemma~\ref{lem_filter} is
nonzero only when $\beta_{1} \equiv 4 \pmod{5}$.
Together with $\beta_{0}+\beta_{1} = 6$ this forces $\beta = (2,4)$, and the
surviving constant is $\tfrac{48}{5} \cdot \tfrac{6!}{2!\,4!} \cdot 5 = 720 =
6!$. The three-variable pattern $(2,2,2)$ of Table~\ref{tab_patterns} is the
same construction with two cube-root factors and nine directions.

\subsection{Apolarity interpretation of the rank formula}\label{sec_apolar}

The rank formula \eqref{eq_rank} has a standard apolar interpretation. For a
homogeneous $F \in \C[\bz]_{p}$, let $F^{\perp}$ denote the ideal of
differential operators that annihilate $F$. A finite subscheme
$X \subset \mathbb{P}^{n}$ is apolar to $F$ when $I_{X}\subset F^{\perp}$.
The apolarity lemma characterizes Waring rank as
\begin{equation}\label{eq_apolar}
    R_{\C}(F) = \min\bigl\{\deg X : X \subset \mathbb{P}^{n} \text{ finite, reduced, and apolar to } F\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
For the monomial $F=\bz^a$, the apolar ideal is the complete intersection
\begin{equation}\label{eq_apolar_ideal}
    (\bz^{a})^{\perp} = \bigl(\partial_{0}^{a_{0}+1}, \ldots, \partial_{n}^{a_{n}+1}\bigr),
\end{equation}
which is an Artinian complete intersection. The step from
\eqref{eq_apolar_ideal} to the lower bound in \eqref{eq_rank} uses the
Hilbert-function argument in~\cite{CCG2012}; the general apolarity framework is
developed in~\cite{IK1999,Landsberg2012}. The linear forms
$\bv_\zeta\cdot\bz$ defined by \eqref{eq_rou} determine
$\prod_{j=1}^{n}(a_j+1)$ distinct projective points. Proposition~\ref{prop_bridge}
and the apolarity lemma identify these points with a reduced apolar scheme of
the degree prescribed by \eqref{eq_rank}.
