\title{Exact Evaluation of High-Order Mixed Derivatives by\\
Rank-Optimal Directional Taylor Jets}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Repeated evaluation of a prescribed high-order mixed derivative can dominate the
cost of neural methods for partial differential equations. We study exact
complex directional formulas that express one order-\(p\) mixed partial as a
weighted sum of order-\(p\) Taylor coefficients along straight lines.
Universal exactness is equivalent to a Waring decomposition over \(\C\) of the
monomial associated with the target multiplicity pattern. The complex Waring
rank therefore gives the minimum number of terms in this formula class, and a
roots-of-unity construction provides an explicit rank-optimal schedule. When
the derivative tensor is real, conjugate terms can be paired so that only one
Taylor jet is evaluated per conjugation orbit. For the patterns \((2,2)\),
\((4,2)\), and \((2,2,2)\), the complex ranks are \(3\), \(5\), and \(9\),
whereas the paired implementation uses \(2\), \(3\), and \(5\) jet calls.
Each directional coefficient is evaluated by normalized Taylor-mode
propagation, and a custom vector--Jacobian product keeps the result
differentiable with respect to model parameters. The numerical study uses
nested automatic differentiation as the sole reference implementation. The
network, residual, sampling points, initialization, optimizer, and precision
are held fixed, so the derivative backend is the only changed component. The
optimality result is restricted to complex straight-line directional formulas;
algebraic term counts and jet-call counts are not interpreted as wall-clock
speedups without controlled timing and memory measurements.
\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.}
High-order automatic differentiation; Taylor-mode differentiation; complex
Waring decomposition; mixed partial derivatives; physics-informed neural
networks.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{MSC 2020.} 65D25, 68T07, 14A25, 35J30.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% =========================================================

Neural methods for partial differential equations now include stochastic,
variational, and residual formulations
\cite{EHanJentzen2017,DeepRitz2018,DGM2018,Raissi2019,Karniadakis2021,Cuomo2022}.
In a physics-informed neural network (PINN), each training step evaluates the
coordinate derivatives required by the differential operator and then
differentiates the residual loss with respect to the model parameters
\cite{Raissi2019,DeepXDE2021}. When the residual contains a prescribed fourth-
or sixth-order mixed partial, successive applications of automatic
differentiation construct nested derivative graphs. High-order equations make
this burden particularly visible; one alternative is to reformulate the PDE as
a lower-order mixed system rather than compute the original high-order
operator directly \cite{Lyu2022MIM}.

Automatic differentiation and its forward- and reverse-mode constructions are
standard tools in scientific computing and machine learning
\cite{Griewank2008,Baydin2018,Pearlmutter1994}. Taylor-mode automatic
differentiation propagates a truncated univariate Taylor series through the
computational graph \cite{Bettencourt2019,TaylorDiff2023}. Recent work reduces
derivative cost by exploiting coordinate structure, trace structure, general
Taylor paths, joint propagation of operator coefficients, or
residual-specific kernels
\cite{Leng2024ZCS,Chen2025AD,Li2024forwardLaplacian,Shi2024stde,Dangel2025Collapsed,Cao2026ExplicitKernels}.
Encoding and representation choices can also change the cost and regularity of
coordinate derivatives \cite{Huang2024Hash}. These approaches do not evaluate
the same restricted algebraic object studied here. We use them to delimit the
scope of the present result, but do not combine them in the numerical
comparison.

The outcome of a PINN experiment also depends on the loss formulation,
optimization dynamics, collocation set, and numerical protocol
\cite{Wang2021Gradient,Wang2022NTK,Krishnapriyan2021,Wu2023Sampling,Kharazmi2021,Berrone2022,Hao2024PINNacle}.
For this reason, the experiments compare the proposed backend only with nested
automatic differentiation and keep every other part of the model and training
pipeline unchanged. The comparison is intended to isolate the derivative
backend rather than rank unrelated PINN formulations.

Let \(\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)\) be one prescribed order-\(p\) mixed
partial. We consider identities
\begin{equation}
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)
=
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_r
\mathcal T_p[u](x;v_r),
\qquad
\lambda_r\in\C,
\quad
v_r\in V_{\C},
\label{eq:intro-question}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal T_p[u](x;v_r)\) is the normalized order-\(p\) contraction of
the derivative tensor along the complex direction \(v_r\). The identity is
required to hold for every admissible order-\(p\) tensor. We seek the smallest
number \(R\) of complex straight-line directional Taylor coefficients. General
Taylor paths with nonzero higher-order input coefficients, operator-level graph
transformations, and residual-specific kernels lie outside this formula class.

The answer is algebraic. The target mixed partial is one coefficient of a
homogeneous polynomial in the direction variables. Equation
\eqref{eq:intro-question} is exact precisely when the corresponding monomial is
represented as a weighted sum of \(p\)-th powers of complex linear forms. This
is the homogeneous-polynomial form of a symmetric tensor-rank decomposition
\cite{Comon2008,Landsberg2012}. The minimum value of \(R\) is therefore the
complex Waring rank of that monomial. This rank is known in closed form
\cite{CCG2012}, and a roots-of-unity decomposition yields an explicit minimal
schedule.

The algebraic schedule is combined with normalized Taylor-mode propagation.
Each complex direction produces one Taylor coefficient, and a custom reverse
rule differentiates the result with respect to the model parameters. Complex
directions are interpreted through the complexification of the real-coordinate
derivative tensor; the construction does not require a general real function to
be evaluated at arbitrary complex points. When the derivative tensor is real,
the schedule is closed under complex conjugation. A conjugate pair can then be
evaluated from one complex jet followed by a real-part operation. This reduces
the number of jet calls without changing the complex Waring rank.

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\roman*)]
\item We characterize universally exact complex formulas of the form
      \eqref{eq:intro-question} by power-sum decompositions of the target
      monomial. This identifies the minimum formula length with the complex
      Waring rank.
\item We convert the known complex monomial decomposition into an explicit
      roots-of-unity derivative schedule and derive a conjugate-orbit
      implementation for real derivative tensors, including its exact jet-call
      count.
\item We implement the schedule with normalized Taylor jets and a custom
      vector--Jacobian product. Numerical verification and performance tests use
      nested automatic differentiation as the only reference and change only
      the derivative backend.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the coefficient-extraction problem.
Section~\ref{sec:waring} gives the complex power-sum characterization, the
rank-optimal roots-of-unity schedule, and the conjugate-orbit reduction.
Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes Taylor-jet evaluation and reverse
differentiation. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} specifies the controlled
comparison with nested automatic differentiation, and
Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss the scope and
limitations of the method.

\section{Problem Statement and Coefficient Extraction}
\label{sec:problem}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Target derivative and active coordinates}

Let \(u:\R^d\to\C\) be \(p\) times continuously differentiable with respect
to its real coordinates in a neighborhood of \(x\); real-valued functions are
included as a special case. A prescribed order-\(p\) derivative involves only
a subset of the coordinates. We denote the active coordinates by
\[
I=(i_1,\ldots,i_s)
\]
and their positive multiplicities by
\[
\boldsymbol a=(a_1,\ldots,a_s),\qquad
1\le a_1\le\cdots\le a_s,\qquad
p=\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_j.
\]
The ordering is by multiplicity; ties may be resolved by the coordinate index.
The target derivative is
\begin{equation}
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)
:=
\partial_{i_1}^{a_1}\cdots\partial_{i_s}^{a_s}u(x).
\label{eq:target-derivative}
\end{equation}
We use
\[
\boldsymbol a!:=\prod_{j=1}^{s}a_j!,
\qquad
\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a}:=\prod_{j=1}^{s}z_j^{a_j}.
\]
Only the active coordinate space
\begin{equation}
V:=\Span\{e_{i_1},\ldots,e_{i_s}\}\subset\R^d
\label{eq:active-space}
\end{equation}
is relevant. Its complexification is
\(V_{\C}:=V\otimes_{\R}\C\).

For \(v\in V_{\C}\), define the normalized order-\(p\) directional Taylor
coefficient by
\begin{equation}
\Tcoef[u](x;v)
:=
\frac{1}{p!}D^p u(x)_{\C}[v,\ldots,v],
\label{eq:taylor-coefficient}
\end{equation}
where \(D^p u(x)_{\C}\) is the complex-multilinear extension of the
real-coordinate derivative tensor. When \(v\in V\), this reduces to
\[
\Tcoef[u](x;v)
=
\frac{1}{p!}\frac{d^p}{d\tau^p}u(x+\tau v)\Big|_{\tau=0}.
\]
Thus, complex directions are tensor contractions; a general real function need
not be defined on a complex neighborhood of \(x\).

\subsection{Generating polynomial}

For \(\boldsymbol z=(z_1,\ldots,z_s)\in\C^s\), define
\begin{equation}
G_x(\boldsymbol z)
:=
\Tcoef[u]\left(x;\sum_{j=1}^{s}z_j e_{i_j}\right).
\label{eq:generating-polynomial}
\end{equation}
The polynomial \(G_x\in\C[\boldsymbol z]_p\) is homogeneous of degree \(p\).

\begin{lemma}[Generating polynomial]
\label{lem:generating-polynomial}
For every \(u\in C^p\) near \(x\),
\begin{equation}
G_x(\boldsymbol z)
=
\sum_{|\boldsymbol b|=p}
\frac{\partial_I^{\boldsymbol b}u(x)}{\boldsymbol b!}
\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol b},
\label{eq:generating-expansion}
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol b=(b_1,\ldots,b_s)\in\N_0^s\),
\(|\boldsymbol b|=\sum_j b_j\), and
\(\partial_I^{\boldsymbol b}
=\partial_{i_1}^{b_1}\cdots\partial_{i_s}^{b_s}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Apply the multinomial theorem to the complexification of the symmetric
multilinear form \(D^p u(x)\):
\[
D^p u(x)_{\C}\left[
\sum_j z_j e_{i_j},\ldots,\sum_j z_j e_{i_j}
\right]
=
\sum_{|\boldsymbol b|=p}
\frac{p!}{\boldsymbol b!}
\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol b}
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol b}u(x).
\]
Division by \(p!\) gives \eqref{eq:generating-expansion}.
\end{proof}

It follows that
\begin{equation}
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)
=
\boldsymbol a!\,[\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a}]G_x(\boldsymbol z),
\label{eq:coefficient-extraction}
\end{equation}
where \([\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a}]\) denotes coefficient extraction.

\subsection{Complex straight-line directional formulas}

\begin{definition}[Complex straight-line directional formula]
\label{def:directional-formula}
A complex directional formula of length \(R\) for
\(\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}\) is a collection
\[
(\lambda_r,v_r)_{r=1}^{R}
\subset \C\times V_{\C}
\]
such that
\begin{equation}
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)
=
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_r\,\Tcoef[u](x;v_r)
\label{eq:directional-formula}
\end{equation}
for every complex symmetric order-\(p\) tensor on the active coordinate space.
\end{definition}

Universal exactness reduces \eqref{eq:directional-formula} to a
finite-dimensional coefficient identity. The next section identifies that
identity and its minimum complex length.

% =========================================================
\section{Complex Rank-Optimal Directional Formulas}
\label{sec:waring}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Power-sum characterization}

For
\(v=\sum_{j=1}^{s}v_j e_{i_j}\in V_{\C}\), write
\[
v\cdot\boldsymbol z:=\sum_{j=1}^{s}v_j z_j.
\]

\begin{definition}[Complex Waring rank]
For a homogeneous polynomial \(F\in\C[\boldsymbol z]_p\), its complex Waring
rank is
\[
R_{\C}(F)
:=
\min\left\{
R:
F=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_r L_r(\boldsymbol z)^p,
\quad
\lambda_r\in\C,
\quad
L_r\in\C[\boldsymbol z]_1
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Power-sum characterization]
\label{thm:bridge}
For
\((\lambda_r,v_r)_{r=1}^{R}\subset
\C\times V_{\C}\), the following statements are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item The directional identity \eqref{eq:directional-formula} holds for every
      complex symmetric order-\(p\) tensor on the active coordinate space.
\item The polynomial identity
      \begin{equation}
      p!\,\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a}
      =
      \sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_r(v_r\cdot\boldsymbol z)^p
      \quad\text{in }\C[\boldsymbol z]_p
      \label{eq:waring-identity}
      \end{equation}
      holds.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:generating-polynomial},
\[
\Tcoef[u](x;v_r)
=
\sum_{|\boldsymbol b|=p}
\frac{\partial_I^{\boldsymbol b}u(x)}{\boldsymbol b!}
 v_r^{\boldsymbol b}.
\]
Because the active complex order-\(p\) tensor is arbitrary,
\eqref{eq:directional-formula} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_r v_r^{\boldsymbol b}
=
\boldsymbol a!\,\delta_{\boldsymbol a\boldsymbol b},
\qquad |\boldsymbol b|=p.
\label{eq:moment-conditions}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of \(\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol b}\) in the right-hand side
of \eqref{eq:waring-identity} is
\[
\binom{p}{\boldsymbol b}
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_r v_r^{\boldsymbol b}.
\]
Coefficient comparison, together with
\(p!/\binom{p}{\boldsymbol b}=\boldsymbol b!\), gives exactly
\eqref{eq:moment-conditions}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:minimum-rank}
The minimum length of a universally exact complex directional formula for
\(\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}\) is
\(R_{\C}(\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a})\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Scope of optimality]
\label{rem:scope-optimality}
Corollary~\ref{cor:minimum-rank} optimizes complex formulas that use
order-\(p\) coefficients of straight-line directions. It does not optimize
over general Taylor paths
\(x+\tau v_1+\tau^2 v_2+\cdots\), methods that propagate several operator
coefficients jointly, or residual-specific symbolic kernels. Those methods use
a different computational class.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Explicit complex schedule}

The complex Waring rank of a monomial is known exactly.

\begin{theorem}[Carlini--Catalisano--Geramita \cite{CCG2012}]
\label{thm:ccg}
Let \(1\le a_1\le\cdots\le a_s\). Then
\begin{equation}
R_{\C}(\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a})
=
\prod_{j=2}^{s}(a_j+1)
=
\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{s}(a_j+1)}{a_1+1}.
\label{eq:complex-rank}
\end{equation}
For \(s=1\), the empty product equals \(1\).
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:ccg} supplies the lower bound for the number of complex
straight-line jets. The next construction attains it and provides a schedule
that can be used directly in an implementation.

\begin{lemma}[Roots-of-unity filter]
\label{lem:root-filter}
For \(m\in\N\) and \(k\in\mathbb Z\),
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\zeta^m=1}\zeta^k
=
m\,\mathbf 1_{\{m\mid k\}}.
\label{eq:root-filter}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If \(m\mid k\), every term equals \(1\). Otherwise, the sum is a nontrivial
finite geometric series and vanishes.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Roots-of-unity schedule]
\label{thm:roots-schedule}
For \(j=2,\ldots,s\), let \(m_j=a_j+1\), and define
\[
\Omega
:=
\left\{
\boldsymbol\zeta=(\zeta_2,\ldots,\zeta_s):
\zeta_j^{m_j}=1
\right\},
\qquad
R:=|\Omega|=\prod_{j=2}^{s}m_j.
\]
For each \(\boldsymbol\zeta\in\Omega\), set
\begin{equation}
v_{\boldsymbol\zeta}
=
e_{i_1}+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\zeta_j e_{i_j},
\qquad
\lambda_{\boldsymbol\zeta}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol a!}{R}\prod_{j=2}^{s}\zeta_j.
\label{eq:roots-schedule}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)
=
\sum_{\boldsymbol\zeta\in\Omega}
\lambda_{\boldsymbol\zeta}\,
\Tcoef[u](x;v_{\boldsymbol\zeta}).
\label{eq:roots-directional-formula}
\end{equation}
The formula has length
\(R=R_{\C}(\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a})\) and is therefore minimal among
complex straight-line directional formulas.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}, it is enough to prove
\[
p!\,\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a}
=
\sum_{\boldsymbol\zeta\in\Omega}
\lambda_{\boldsymbol\zeta}
(v_{\boldsymbol\zeta}\cdot\boldsymbol z)^p.
\]
For \(|\boldsymbol b|=p\), the coefficient of
\(\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol b}\) on the right-hand side is
\[
\frac{\boldsymbol a!}{R}
\binom{p}{\boldsymbol b}
\prod_{j=2}^{s}
\left(
\sum_{\zeta_j^{m_j}=1}\zeta_j^{b_j+1}
\right).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:root-filter}, this coefficient vanishes unless
\(b_j\equiv a_j\pmod{a_j+1}\) for every \(j\ge2\). Hence
\[
b_j=a_j+t_j(a_j+1),\qquad t_j\in\N_0.
\]
If some \(t_j>0\), then
\[
b_1
=
a_1-\sum_{j=2}^{s}t_j(a_j+1)<0,
\]
which is impossible because \(a_j\ge a_1\). Thus
\(\boldsymbol b=\boldsymbol a\) is the only surviving index. For this index,
the root sums cancel \(R\), and the coefficient is
\[
\boldsymbol a!\binom{p}{\boldsymbol a}=p!.
\]
This proves the polynomial identity. Minimality follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:ccg}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Meaning of complex directions]
\label{rem:complex-directions}
For a general real \(C^p\) function, equation
\eqref{eq:roots-directional-formula} is an identity in the complexification of
\(D^p u(x)\); it is not an instruction to evaluate \(u\) at arbitrary complex
points. A direct complex-jet implementation realizes the same tensor
contractions when all computational primitives support the required complex
derivatives.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Conjugate-orbit evaluation for real derivative tensors}
\label{sec:conjugate-orbits}

The roots-of-unity set \(\Omega\) is invariant under componentwise complex
conjugation. Moreover,
\[
v_{\overline{\boldsymbol\zeta}}
=
\overline{v_{\boldsymbol\zeta}},
\qquad
\lambda_{\overline{\boldsymbol\zeta}}
=
\overline{\lambda_{\boldsymbol\zeta}}.
\]
This symmetry reduces the number of jet evaluations when the derivative tensor
is real.

\begin{proposition}[Conjugate-orbit formula]
\label{prop:conjugate-orbits}
Assume that \(D^p u(x)\) is real. Let
\[
\Omega_0
:=
\{\boldsymbol\zeta\in\Omega:
  \overline{\boldsymbol\zeta}=\boldsymbol\zeta\},
\]
and choose one representative from each two-element conjugate orbit to form
\(\Omega_+\). Then
\begin{equation}
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)
=
\sum_{\boldsymbol\zeta\in\Omega_0}
\lambda_{\boldsymbol\zeta}\,
\Tcoef[u](x;v_{\boldsymbol\zeta})
+
2\operatorname{Re}
\sum_{\boldsymbol\zeta\in\Omega_+}
\lambda_{\boldsymbol\zeta}\,
\Tcoef[u](x;v_{\boldsymbol\zeta}).
\label{eq:conjugate-orbit-formula}
\end{equation}
The number of Taylor-jet evaluations is
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{orb}}
=
\frac{R+F}{2},
\qquad
F
=
\prod_{j=2}^{s}\gcd(2,a_j+1),
\label{eq:orbit-count}
\end{equation}
with the empty product interpreted as one.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Real multilinearity gives
\(
\Tcoef[u](x;\overline v)
=
\overline{\Tcoef[u](x;v)}
\).
Hence the two terms indexed by
\(\boldsymbol\zeta\) and
\(\overline{\boldsymbol\zeta}\) sum to
\(2\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{\boldsymbol\zeta}
\Tcoef[u](x;v_{\boldsymbol\zeta}))\).
A root of unity is fixed by conjugation exactly when it is real. The equation
\(\zeta^{a_j+1}=1\) has \(\gcd(2,a_j+1)\) real solutions, so
\(|\Omega_0|=F\). The remaining \(R-F\) elements form two-element orbits,
which gives \eqref{eq:orbit-count}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Equation~\eqref{eq:conjugate-orbit-formula} is a real-linear implementation
identity for real derivative tensors. It does not give a shorter complex Waring
decomposition and therefore does not alter Theorem~\ref{thm:ccg}. A complex jet
call is also not assumed to have the same cost as a real jet call.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Examples}

\begin{example}[Second-order cross derivative]
\label{ex:second-order}
For \(\boldsymbol a=(1,1)\), the schedule has two directions:
\[
\partial_{i_1}\partial_{i_2}u(x)
=
\frac12\Tcoef[u](x;e_{i_1}+e_{i_2})
-
\frac12\Tcoef[u](x;e_{i_1}-e_{i_2}).
\]
The associated polynomial identity is
\[
2z_1z_2
=
\frac12(z_1+z_2)^2-
\frac12(z_1-z_2)^2.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Sixth-order pattern \((2,2,2)\)]
\label{ex:222}
Let
\[
\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u
=
\partial_{i_1}^2\partial_{i_2}^2\partial_{i_3}^2u,
\qquad
\boldsymbol a=(2,2,2).
\]
With \(\omega=e^{2\pi\mathrm i/3}\) and
\(U_3=\{1,\omega,\omega^2\}\), Theorem~\ref{thm:roots-schedule} gives nine
directions
\[
v_{\zeta_2,\zeta_3}
=
e_{i_1}+\zeta_2e_{i_2}+\zeta_3e_{i_3},
\qquad
(\zeta_2,\zeta_3)\in U_3^2,
\]
and weights
\[
\lambda_{\zeta_2,\zeta_3}
=
\frac{8}{9}\zeta_2\zeta_3.
\]
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\partial_{i_1}^2\partial_{i_2}^2\partial_{i_3}^2u(x)
=
\frac{8}{9}
\sum_{\zeta_2,\zeta_3\in U_3}
\zeta_2\zeta_3\,
\Tcoef[u]\left(
x;e_{i_1}+\zeta_2e_{i_2}+\zeta_3e_{i_3}
\right).
\label{eq:example-222}
\end{equation}
The complex rank is \(9\). The unique fixed conjugation index is
\((\zeta_2,\zeta_3)=(1,1)\); the other eight indices form four conjugate pairs.
Equation~\eqref{eq:conjugate-orbit-formula} therefore evaluates the same real
derivative with five jet calls.
\end{example}

\section{Taylor-Jet Evaluation and Differentiation}
\label{sec:implementation}
% =========================================================

The previous section determines a set of directions and weights. This section describes the evaluation of each \(\Tcoef[u](x;v)\), the
reverse derivative with respect to network parameters, and the computational
quantities used in the backend comparison.

\subsection{Normalized Taylor jets}

For a fixed direction \(v\), consider the path
\[
x(\tau)=x+\tau v.
\]
Each intermediate quantity is represented by a normalized truncated Taylor
series
\[
y(\tau)
=
\sum_{k=0}^{p}y_k\tau^k+O(\tau^{p+1}),
\qquad
y_k=\frac{1}{k!}\frac{d^k y}{d\tau^k}(0).
\]
The output coefficient \(y_p\) is \(\Tcoef[u](x;v)\).

For an affine map \(y=Wx+b\),
\begin{equation}
y_0=Wx_0+b,
\qquad
y_k=Wx_k,\quad k=1,\ldots,p.
\label{eq:jet-affine}
\end{equation}
For an entrywise activation \(y=\phi(x)\), write
\[
q(\tau)=\phi'(x(\tau))=\sum_{k=0}^{p}q_k\tau^k.
\]
The identity \(y'=q x'\) gives
\begin{equation}
y_k
=
\frac{1}{k}
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)q_jx_{k-j},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,p.
\label{eq:jet-general}
\end{equation}
The coefficients \(q_j\) are obtained from the activation-specific
recurrence.

For \(y=\tanh(x)\), \(q=1-y^2\), and
\begin{equation}
q_0=1-y_0^2,
\qquad
q_k=-\sum_{j=0}^{k}y_jy_{k-j},
\quad k\ge1.
\label{eq:jet-tanh}
\end{equation}
For \(y=\sinh(x)\), introduce \(c=\cosh(x)\). Then
\begin{align}
y_k
&=
\frac{1}{k}
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)c_jx_{k-j},
\label{eq:jet-sinh}\\
c_k
&=
\frac{1}{k}
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-j)y_jx_{k-j},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,p.
\label{eq:jet-cosh}
\end{align}
These recurrences are algebraic and apply to real or complex coefficient
tensors. The functions \(\sinh\) and \(\cosh\) are entire. The function
\(\tanh\) is meromorphic; its local recurrence is valid away from its poles,
but it does not define a globally entire complex network.

\subsection{Reverse differentiation through a jet}

Differentiating through every scalar operation in the recurrence creates an
unnecessarily large reverse graph. We instead use the linearized identity
\(\delta y=q\,\delta x\) in the truncated-series algebra. Let \(\bar y_k\)
be the cotangent of \(y_k\). Under the complex cotangent convention used by
our implementation, the activation pullback is
\begin{equation}
\bar x_m
=
\sum_{j=0}^{p-m}\overline{q_j}\,\bar y_{m+j},
\qquad m=0,\ldots,p.
\label{eq:jet-vjp}
\end{equation}
The conjugation convention is framework dependent. The implementation is
therefore checked by both finite-difference directional tests and complex
inner-product tests, rather than by value comparisons alone. Equation
\eqref{eq:jet-vjp} stores \(p+1\) coefficient tensors per retained activation
and avoids reverse differentiation through the individual recurrence steps.

\subsection{Reference and proposed backends}
\label{sec:backends}

The numerical study compares two derivative backends for a fixed target
\(\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)\).

\paragraph{Nested automatic differentiation.}
The reference implementation uses PyTorch reverse-mode automatic
differentiation \cite{Paszke2019}. It differentiates successively along an
ordered list of \(p\) coordinate indices and retains the graph so that the
residual remains differentiable with respect to model parameters.

\paragraph{Proposed Waring--Taylor backend.}
The proposed backend uses the rank-optimal roots-of-unity schedule in
\eqref{eq:roots-directional-formula}. For a general complex derivative tensor,
it evaluates all
\[
R_{\C}(\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a})
=
\prod_{j=2}^{s}(a_j+1)
\]
terms. When the derivative tensor is real, it uses the conjugate-orbit formula
\eqref{eq:conjugate-orbit-formula} and evaluates one representative per orbit.
Nonreal directions are propagated by the complex Taylor-jet rules, and the
weighted result remains differentiable through the custom reverse rule.

Both backends return the same target derivative in exact arithmetic. The
comparison changes only how that derivative is evaluated; it does not change
the model architecture, residual, sampling points, initialization, optimizer,
or training schedule.

\subsection{End-to-end proposed schedule}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:waring-jet} summarizes the full complex schedule.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Proposed complex Waring--Taylor backend}
\label{alg:waring-jet}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE model \(u\); point \(x\in\R^d\); active coordinates
         \(I=(i_1,\ldots,i_s)\); multiplicities
         \(1\le a_1\le\cdots\le a_s\)
\ENSURE \(\partial_I^{\boldsymbol a}u(x)\)
\STATE \(p\leftarrow\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_j\)
\STATE \(m_j\leftarrow a_j+1\) for \(j=2,\ldots,s\)
\STATE \(\Omega\leftarrow
       \{(\zeta_2,\ldots,\zeta_s):\zeta_j^{m_j}=1\}\)
\STATE \(R\leftarrow|\Omega|\), \(\mathrm{result}\leftarrow0\)
\FORALL{\(\boldsymbol\zeta\in\Omega\)}
  \STATE \(v\leftarrow e_{i_1}+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\zeta_j e_{i_j}\)
  \STATE \(\lambda\leftarrow
         (\boldsymbol a!/R)\prod_{j=2}^{s}\zeta_j\)
  \STATE initialize \((x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_p)\leftarrow(x,v,0,\ldots,0)\)
  \STATE propagate the jet through \(u\) using
         \eqref{eq:jet-affine}--\eqref{eq:jet-cosh}
  \STATE \(\mathrm{result}\leftarrow
         \mathrm{result}+\lambda y_p\)
\ENDFOR
\RETURN \(\mathrm{result}\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Theorem~\ref{thm:roots-schedule} establishes algebraic exactness. For a real derivative tensor,
the loop is taken over \(\Omega_0\cup\Omega_+\); fixed indices contribute once,
and nonfixed representatives contribute through twice the real part as in
\eqref{eq:conjugate-orbit-formula}. Numerical error is introduced only by
finite-precision jet propagation and weighted summation.

\subsection{Cost model and stability diagnostics}

Let \(C_{\mathrm{lin}}\) denote the cost of one forward pass through all affine
maps for one coefficient tensor, and let \(N_{\mathrm{act}}\) be the number of
scalar activation entries processed in one network evaluation. With the
recurrences written above, one straight-line jet has the value-evaluation cost
\begin{equation}
O\!\left(p C_{\mathrm{lin}}+p^2N_{\mathrm{act}}\right).
\label{eq:jet-cost}
\end{equation}
Let \(R_{\mathrm{call}}\) denote the number of evaluated jets. It equals
\(R_{\C}\) for the full complex schedule and \(R_{\mathrm{orb}}\) for the
conjugate-orbit implementation on a real derivative tensor. Sequential evaluation
multiplies the single-jet cost by \(R_{\mathrm{call}}\) while keeping storage
proportional to \(p\) coefficient tensors per retained activation. Fully batched
evaluation exposes parallelism but increases coefficient storage proportionally
to \(R_{\mathrm{call}}\). The constants differ between real and complex
arithmetic and between value-only and parameter-gradient evaluations. Equation
\eqref{eq:jet-cost} is therefore a cost model, not a runtime result.

For each weighted sum, we report the cancellation indicator
\begin{equation}
\kappa_{\mathrm{sum}}
:=
\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{R}|\lambda_r y_{p,r}|}
     {\max\!\left(\left|\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_r y_{p,r}\right|,
                  \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\right)}.
\label{eq:cancellation-indicator}
\end{equation}
Large values identify cases in which rounding error can be amplified by
cancellation. For real-valued targets evaluated with the full complex schedule, the normalized
imaginary residual
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{im}}
:=
\frac{|\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{result})|}
     {\max(|\operatorname{Re}(\mathrm{result})|,
           \varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}})}
\label{eq:imaginary-residual}
\end{equation}
provides an additional implementation and stability diagnostic.

\section{Numerical Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}
% =========================================================

The numerical study compares only nested automatic differentiation and the
proposed Waring--Taylor backend. This two-backend design is deliberate: it
isolates the effect of replacing the derivative calculation and avoids mixing
that effect with changes in the model architecture, operator representation, or
training procedure. The reporting protocol follows the emphasis on fixed
problem definitions, complete hyperparameters, and machine-readable benchmark
records used in scientific-learning software and benchmark studies
\cite{DeepXDE2021,Hao2024PINNacle}.

\subsection{Verification setup}

The executable suite uses double precision for real tests and complex128 when
complex propagation is required. It covers pure, repeated-index, and
square-free patterns through order six, including \((2,2)\), \((4,2)\),
\((2,2,2)\), and \((1^6)\). For every test, nested automatic differentiation
produces the reference derivative and parameter gradient. The proposed backend
uses the full complex schedule for a complex derivative tensor and the paired
conjugate-orbit implementation for a real derivative tensor. Every output is an
ordinary tensor, after which the residual is differentiated with respect to the
same model parameters.

For a reference derivative \(d_{\mathrm{AD}}\), the proposed derivative
\(d_{\mathrm{WT}}\), and their parameter gradients, we report
\begin{equation}
E_d
=
\frac{|d_{\mathrm{WT}}-d_{\mathrm{AD}}|}
     {\max(|d_{\mathrm{AD}}|,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}})},
\qquad
E_g
=
\frac{\|g_{\mathrm{WT}}-g_{\mathrm{AD}}\|_2}
     {\max(\|g_{\mathrm{AD}}\|_2,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}})}.
\label{eq:verification-errors}
\end{equation}
Complex pullbacks are checked independently by an adjoint inner-product
identity. The cancellation indicator \eqref{eq:cancellation-indicator} and,
for the full complex schedule applied to a real target, the imaginary residual
\eqref{eq:imaginary-residual} are recorded as stability diagnostics.

\subsection{Controlled PDE training comparison}

PDE experiments use one fixed network architecture for each problem. Paired
runs start from identical parameters and use the same collocation points,
minibatch order, residual weights, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, stopping
rule, and evaluation grid. Because the residual-point distribution can itself
change PINN accuracy, each pair reuses exactly the same sampled points
\cite{Wu2023Sampling}. The paired runs use AdamW, the decoupled-weight-decay
variant of Adam, with identical hyperparameters for both derivative backends
\cite{KingmaBa2015,LoshchilovHutter2019}. No optimizer switch is made within a
timed run. The only difference is whether the prescribed high-order derivative
is evaluated by nested automatic differentiation or by the proposed
Waring--Taylor backend. We report solution error as a function of both
iteration count and wall time, together with total training time and peak
memory. Existing experiments that change the architecture, activation, or
representation are excluded from this comparison because they do not isolate
the derivative backend.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Formula class and scope of optimality}

The main result concerns one prescribed monomial derivative and one complex
formula class: weighted sums of order-\(p\) Taylor coefficients along
straight-line directions. Within this class, the minimum number of terms is the
complex Waring rank. The result is independent of the network architecture. It
does not provide a lower bound for general Taylor paths with higher-order input
coefficients, methods that propagate several operator terms jointly, or
residual-specific derivative kernels. The paper therefore makes no claim of
numerical superiority over those broader approaches.

This scope also determines the experimental design. Nested automatic
differentiation is used as the sole reference because it evaluates the same
target derivative without changing the operator or model. This choice is also
consistent with recent analyses that treat automatic differentiation as a
central component of neural PDE training rather than as an interchangeable
finite-difference approximation \cite{Chen2025AD}. The resulting comparison
answers a specific question: what changes when the derivative backend is
replaced by the proposed complex Waring--Taylor construction?

\subsection{Conjugate pairing and call counts}

The rank-optimal complex decomposition contains \(R_{\C}\) powers. For a real
derivative tensor, Equation~\eqref{eq:conjugate-orbit-formula} evaluates this
same complex decomposition with one jet per conjugation orbit. This reduction
uses conjugate symmetry and real-linear postprocessing; it is not a different
Waring decomposition. A complex-valued target generally requires the full
schedule. Moreover, one complex jet call need not have the same cost as one
automatic-differentiation sweep, so \(R_{\mathrm{orb}}\) is not a speedup
factor.

\subsection{Numerical stability and computational cost}

Complex roots of unity lead to oscillatory weighted sums. Finite-precision
error can therefore be amplified even when the formula is exact. The
cancellation indicator \(\kappa_{\mathrm{sum}}\), the imaginary residual of the
full schedule, and direct derivative and adjoint checks expose different
failure modes. The paired formula removes the final imaginary cancellation for
real targets but does not remove cancellation within each real-part
contribution.

Runtime depends on more than the number of directions. Complex kernels have
different throughput and storage requirements from real kernels. Batching
reduces launch overhead but increases activation storage; sequential evaluation
reduces memory but exposes less parallelism. Parameter back-propagation also
includes the custom reverse rule. Consequently, efficacy, timing, and memory
claims require the controlled two-backend measurements specified in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Controlled PDE interpretation}

The PDE comparison changes only the derivative backend. This removes the
architecture and representation confounders that arise when different network
classes are compared, but it also narrows the conclusion. Such controls are
important because PINN training can be strongly affected by gradient imbalance,
loss conditioning, and optimization pathologies
\cite{Wang2021Gradient,Wang2022NTK,Krishnapriyan2021}. A lower error at a fixed
iteration count would indicate a numerical difference between derivative
implementations; a lower error at a fixed wall time would additionally reflect
their computational cost. Neither result would establish superiority over
methods that reformulate the operator or use a different derivative class.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The custom forward and reverse rules make executable validation essential.
Each numerical result is tied to a versioned bundle containing the source
commit, random seed, hardware, software environment, precision, architecture,
derivative pattern, sampling protocol, optimizer settings, and evaluation
procedure. Tables and figures are generated directly from these bundles rather
than transcribed manually, following the reproducibility emphasis of modern
PINN software and benchmark suites \cite{DeepXDE2021,Hao2024PINNacle}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =========================================================

We have characterized universally exact complex straight-line directional
formulas for a prescribed high-order mixed derivative. The characterization is
a power-sum identity for the associated monomial, so the minimum number of terms
is its complex Waring rank. Combining the known monomial-rank formula with a
roots-of-unity decomposition gives an explicit rank-optimal complex schedule.

For real derivative tensors, conjugate terms can be evaluated by one jet per
conjugation orbit. This reduces the jet-call counts for \((2,2)\), \((4,2)\),
and \((2,2,2)\) from the complex ranks \(3\), \(5\), and \(9\) to \(2\),
\(3\), and \(5\), respectively. The paired formula evaluates the same complex
decomposition and does not change its rank.

Normalized Taylor propagation evaluates each directional coefficient, and a
custom vector--Jacobian product preserves differentiation with respect to model
parameters. The numerical study compares this backend only with nested
automatic differentiation while holding the model, residual, data, and training
procedure fixed. This design isolates the derivative backend. Practical speed
and memory advantages remain empirical claims and must be supported by the
controlled end-to-end measurements specified in the paper.

\section{Apolar Interpretation of the Rank Formula}
\label{app:apolarity}

For completeness, we recall the algebraic object underlying
Theorem~\ref{thm:ccg}. Let
\(F=\boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol a}\in\C[\boldsymbol z]_p\), and let
\(\C[\boldsymbol\partial]\) act on \(\C[\boldsymbol z]\) by
differentiation. The apolar ideal of \(F\) is
\begin{equation}
F^\perp
=
\left(
\partial_1^{a_1+1},\ldots,\partial_s^{a_s+1}
\right).
\label{eq:apolar-ideal}
\end{equation}
It is a complete intersection. The apolarity lemma identifies a Waring
decomposition of \(F\) with a reduced finite set of projective points whose
vanishing ideal is contained in \(F^\perp\). The Hilbert function of
\(\C[\boldsymbol\partial]/F^\perp\) gives the lower bound
\[
R_{\C}(F)\ge \prod_{j=2}^{s}(a_j+1),
\]
and the roots-of-unity construction in
Theorem~\ref{thm:roots-schedule} attains this bound. Detailed treatments are
given in \cite{IK1999,CCG2012,Landsberg2012}.
